2. **Discussing Personal Issues**: Students may casually mention that they're discussing personal issues or seeking advice from a chatbot, which could indicate they're using it as a primary source of emotional support.
    
3. **Anthropomorphizing the Chatbot**: If a student starts referring to a chatbot as if it were a real person, attributing emotions or intentions to it, this could suggest an unhealthy attachment.
    
4. **Emotional Distress**: Noticeable signs of emotional distress that don't seem to be alleviated by human interaction, but rather by time spent with chatbots, might be a red flag.
    
5. **Avoidance of Professional Help**: A student might be reluctant to seek help from a counselor or therapist, insisting that the chatbot is all the support they need.
    

How to Provide Guidance:

1. **Educate About AI Limitations**: Teach students about the limitations of AI chatbots and how they are not substitutes for human connection or professional mental health support.
    
2. **Promote Healthy Relationships**: Encourage students to build and maintain healthy relationships with peers and trusted adults. Emphasize the importance of face-to-face interactions for emotional support and development.
    
3. **Develop Emotional Intelligence**: Incorporate emotional intelligence and resilience-building into the curriculum. Teach students strategies for managing their emotions and coping with stress.
    
4. **Create Awareness**: Hold workshops or assemblies to discuss the emotional implications of over relying on technology for companionship or support. Share stories and examples to illustrate the potential risks and healthier alternatives.
    
5. **Parental Engagement**: Involve parents by informing them about the potential for overreliance on chatbots for emotional support. Encourage them to talk with their children about their online interactions and to foster open communication at home.
    
6. **Encourage Real-World Activities**: Promote extracurricular activities that encourage real-world engagement, such as clubs, sports, arts, and volunteer work. These activities can provide a sense of community and belonging that chatbots cannot replicate.

When working with teenagers in particular, educators and guardians must operate under the assumption that these young individuals are actively engaging with social media and conversational AI platforms like chatbots. It's crucial to be proactive in educating them about the potential lack of privacy in these interactions. You should emphasize that the information they share with these bots can potentially be accessed by the companies that own the platforms. It's important to instill a sense of caution by encouraging students to consider the implications of their digital conversations being exposed to parents, peers, or other unintended audiences. By helping students to vividly imagine such scenarios, they can better grasp the importance of being mindful and responsible in their use of these technologies.

# 8.3 Mitigating Risks in AI Use

As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly integrated into educational environments, it is imperative to address the potential risks and challenges that accompany its use. In this section, we will explore the concept of risk mitigation in the context of AI applications within the classroom. Mitigating risk involves implementing strategies to minimize the negative impacts that AI could have on students, educators, and the broader school community. These impacts range from concerns over data privacy and security to the ethical implications of AI's influence on learning and student well-being.

The goal of risk mitigation is not to stifle innovation or discourage the use of AI in education but to ensure that its adoption is thoughtful, responsible, and aligned with educational values. We want to proactively address these risks, we can harness the power of AI to enhance learning experiences while safeguarding the interests and rights of all members of the educational community.

### 8.3.1 Importance and Applications of Risk Mitigation in Education

Let's assume you've gone through the GUIDE framework for your use case, and find that it will add value to your students.

After going through the GUIDE framework, you would then assign a risk category to each AI application based on the insights gained. As discussed, the risk categories are typically High, Medium, or Low, and they are determined by factors such as the consequence of error, reversibility, stakeholder impact, frequency of decision, visibility, and historical precedence. This will be an evolving list based on your personal experiences, and will be something you return to as new situations arise.

- **High Risk**: AI applications that could lead to significant negative outcomes, such as those affecting safety, security, or fundamental rights.
- **Medium Risk**: AI applications where the consequences are inconvenient but not severely detrimental.
- **Low Risk**: AI applications where the stakes are minimal, and the consequences are easily reversible or negligible.

### 8.3.2 Key Strategies for Risk Mitigation in AI Use

Imagine Ms. Johnson, a high school history teacher, has been diligent in her efforts to integrate AI into her classroom. She has followed the school district's policies, obtained consent from parents and students, and provided her class with a thorough orientation on the AI tool's capabilities and ethical use. The AI tool she selected is designed to enhance student engagement by providing interactive, personalized learning experiences.

#### The Incident

One day, during a lesson on the American Revolution, Ms. Johnson decides to use the AI tool to simulate historical debates. The students are excited to interact with the AI, which is programmed to respond to their arguments as different historical figures. Initially, the simulation goes well, with students actively participating and the AI providing historically accurate responses.

However, the situation quickly escalates when the AI begins to generate inappropriate and biased content, portraying certain historical figures in a derogatory manner. The students are confused and upset by the AI's responses, and the classroom environment becomes tense. Ms. Johnson immediately stops the simulation and addresses the class, acknowledging the issue and reassuring the students that this was not the intended outcome of the lesson.

#### Debrief
In Ms. Johnson's class, the AI tool was intended to create an immersive and interactive learning experience by simulating historical debates. Despite her thorough preparation and adherence to best practices, the AI quickly derailed, leading to an uncomfortable situation. Let's examine the factors that contributed to this incident:

**Inadequate Content Moderation**
The AI tool may not have had robust content moderation mechanisms in place. Without effective filters or oversight, AI can generate responses that are inappropriate, offensive, or biased. This could be due to the AI's training data containing such biases or the lack of a comprehensive algorithm to screen for sensitive content.

**AI Misinterpretation**
AI operates based on algorithms and data patterns, and it may misinterpret the context or the nuances of human language. In a historical debate, the AI might have taken certain arguments or keywords out of context, leading to responses that were historically inaccurate or culturally insensitive.

**Lack of Contextual Understanding**
AI, especially if not specifically designed for educational purposes, may lack the nuanced understanding of historical events and the sensitivity required to handle them appropriately. It might not distinguish between providing a factual representation of a historical figure's views and endorsing those views, which can be problematic if those views are controversial or offensive by today's standards.

**Training Data Issues**
The AI's training data could have been biased or unrepresentative of the diverse perspectives required for a balanced historical discussion. If the AI was trained on data that included biased or one-sided historical interpretations, it might reproduce those biases in its interactions with students.

**Algorithmic Limitations**
The algorithms driving the AI might not have been sophisticated enough to handle the complexity of a historical debate. AI can struggle with tasks that require deep understanding, critical thinking, or ethical judgment, which are often necessary in educational settings.

**Unanticipated User Interactions**
Students may interact with AI in unpredictable ways, asking questions or making statements that the AI is not prepared to handle appropriately. The AI might not have been designed to manage the wide range of potential inputs from a classroom full of students.

**Technical Glitches**
The incident could have been the result of a technical glitch or a malfunction in the AI's programming. Software bugs or errors can cause AI to behave erratically or produce unexpected results.

In summary, the incident in Ms. Johnson's class likely stemmed from a combination of inadequate content moderation, AI misinterpretation, lack of contextual understanding, issues with training data, algorithmic limitations, unanticipated user interactions, and potential technical glitches. These factors highlight the challenges of implementing AI in complex, nuanced environments like the classroom, where the technology must be sensitive to historical, cultural, and ethical contexts.

#### Mitigation Strategies
So how do we mitigate these risks associated with AI applications in our business? It's essential to develop a comprehensive strategy that addresses the technical, ethical, and regulatory challenges posed by AI technologies. This involves a multi-faceted approach that includes risk assessment, stakeholder engagement, policy development, legal and ethical review, communication, pilot testing, and ongoing monitoring and iteration.

**Transparency and Consent**
Maintain transparency about the AI tools being used and their purposes. Obtain informed consent from students and parents by:

- **Clear Communication**: Explain how AI will be used in the classroom and what data will be collected (if any).
- **Consent Forms**: Provide consent forms that clearly state the terms of AI use and the rights of students and parents.
- **Open Dialogue**: Encourage questions and discussions about AI use to foster an environment of trust and understanding PRIOR to implementation.

#### Education and Training
Equip both students and yourself with the knowledge and skills necessary to use AI responsibly:

- **Student Workshops**: Conduct workshops that teach students about AI capabilities, limitations, and ethical use.
- **Educator Professional Development**: Advocate administration for training sessions, or find resources online for you and your educator peers to stay updated on the latest AI tools and best practices for their use in education.
- **Critical Thinking**: Incorporate lessons that enhance students' critical thinking skills when interacting with AI-generated content.

**Monitoring and Evaluation**
Implement systems to monitor the use of AI tools and evaluate their impact on education:

- **Regular Reviews**: If your school (or you) purchase an AI tool for the classroom, encourage administration to schedule periodic reviews of AI tool usage to ensure they align with educational goals and policies.
- **Feedback Mechanisms**: Create channels for students and educators to provide feedback on AI tools and their effectiveness.
- **Impact Assessment**: Assess the impact of AI on student learning and well-being, making adjustments as necessary. This can be as simple as having students fill out a feedback survey after the use case.

#### Incident Response Planning
Develop protocols for responding to incidents involving AI, such as misuse or technical failures:

- **Incident Response Team**: Form a team responsible for managing AI-related incidents.
- **Reporting System**: Establish a system for reporting and tracking incidents involving AI.
- **Resolution Procedures**: Outline steps for resolving incidents, including communication plans and remedial actions.
#### Policy Development
Develop clear and comprehensive policies that outline acceptable uses of AI in educational settings. These policies should address:

- **Acceptable and Unacceptable Uses**: Define what AI tools can and cannot be used for in the classroom.
- **Data Governance**: Establish rules for data collection, storage, and use, ensuring compliance with privacy laws and regulations.
- **Academic Integrity**: Create guidelines that prevent plagiarism and promote honesty in student work involving AI.
- **Accessibility**: Ensure that AI tools are accessible to all students, including those with disabilities.

Ultimately, despite our best efforts to mitigate risks associated with the use of AI in education, it is impossible to eliminate risk entirely. Each educator, in collaboration with their community, must assess their tolerance for potential risks. This involves a careful consideration of the specific context in which AI tools are to be used, the educational goals at stake, and the level of risk that is deemed acceptable. Educators must weigh the potential benefits of AI integration against the worst-case scenarios, ensuring that the anticipated positive outcomes for students' learning and development justify the risks taken. It is a delicate balance, but with a tiered approach to risk assessment and a clear understanding of both the opportunities and limitations of AI, educators can make informed decisions that prioritize the well-being and growth of their students.
