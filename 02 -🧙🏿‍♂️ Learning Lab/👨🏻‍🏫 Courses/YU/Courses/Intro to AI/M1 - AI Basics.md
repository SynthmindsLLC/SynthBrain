# History of AI

As we embark on this intellectual adventure, it's essential to start at the beginning; because understanding the history of AI isn't just about knowing dates and names, it's about grasping the evolution of ideas, technologies, and philosophies that have shaped this incredible field.

  

AI didn't just appear overnight. It's the result of centuries of human thought, decades of academic research, and years of technological innovation. By tracing its roots, you'll gain invaluable insights into why AI works the way it does today and where it could go in the future.

  

Let's travel back in time and explore the origins of AI.

### Philosophical Foundations

Let's take a step back, way back, to ancient times when philosophers like Plato and Aristotle first pondered the nature of intelligence and reason. Imagine a world where the concept of a 'machine' was as fantastical as dragons and unicorns are today. In this setting, the idea of 'artificial intelligence' was born—not as a technology, but as a philosophical question: "Can a non-human entity think?"

  

Fast forward to the Renaissance, where inventors like Leonardo da Vinci sketched designs for mechanical knights. These automata were the ancestors of modern robots, embodying the dream of creating artificial life. But they were more than just gears and levers; they symbolized the human quest to understand ourselves by replicating our abilities—our intelligence—in a different form.

Then came the Age of Enlightenment, where logic and reason were the stars of the show. Philosophers like René Descartes and Immanuel Kant dissected the nature of thought itself. Their work laid the intellectual groundwork for the computational theories that would later become the backbone of AI.

  

So, why is this important? Because every algorithm, every neural network, and every AI application you'll encounter in this course has its roots in these early philosophical musings. Understanding this history enriches your grasp of AI, making you not just a consumer of this technology but a thoughtful participant in its ongoing story.

### Mathematical and Computational Roots

Now that we've explored the philosophical musings that laid the groundwork for AI, let's fast-forward to the 20th century. Here, the abstract ideas of yesteryears found a concrete playground: Mathematics and Computation.

  

Picture a young Alan Turing, sitting in a dimly lit room, pondering the limits of computation. His groundbreaking work on Turing machines would not only define what could be computed but also lay the foundation for all of computer science. Turing's machines were the first to simulate any computer algorithm, no matter how complex. In essence, he gave us the language to translate human thought into machine operations.

  

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/_9s_oUqTvsuv2HY8PhgRTdi5q7DcJb8x-4PTw4cr06aQBV875Qub5t-nPsWZkO7g8akDULZzawA45891oPRBilTSOvgY7hGsih-eWLGsTZilOyK-Pkvpb_y1Q4nuMKHwvb_FGkNovXECtc8WA2biukw)

Around the same time, John von Neumann was making strides in how these computations could be implemented efficiently. His architecture for an electronic digital computer set the stage for most modern computers and made the dream of general-purpose, programmable machines a reality. While Turing provided the "what" in computation, von Neumann provided the "how."

  

<SIDEBAR: 

Alan Turing was born in London in 1912, and from an early age, he displayed an extraordinary aptitude for mathematics and logic. He studied at King's College, Cambridge, where he developed the concept of a "universal machine" that could compute anything that is computable. This theoretical construct was later named the Turing Machine in his honor.

  

What is a Turing Machine?

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/S4aX9HL2xwB9547qDNwaEe5Ym3RUvLxRcrBU7a-ddtalOyFOtlt9f1nL-Mjmc8TMtJpZBClQs-r0a7JL5myQoOFEB-F4a_GPuUt6KdwdQSikhcFoBvhypOnqiJg6y9hf1rXjeZzFB1zMGvDwQNdJ8UY)

[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_machine)

  

A Turing Machine is a mathematical model of computation that provides an abstract machine capable of simulating any computer algorithm. Imagine a machine with an infinite tape divided into cells. Each cell contains a symbol from a finite alphabet. The machine has a 'head' that can read and write symbols on the tape and move the tape left or right one cell at a time. Based on a set of rules and the symbol it reads, the machine transitions between states, writes a new symbol, and moves its head. This simple yet powerful model serves as the foundation for the theory of computation.

Turing's Early Work

Turing's early work was deeply influenced by the question of decidability—whether a mathematical statement can be proven to be true or false. His 1936 paper, "On Computable Numbers," introduced the concept of a machine that could perform any conceivable mathematical computation if it were represented as an algorithm. This paper is considered one of the foundational works of computer science.

  

Turing's Impact

Turing's ideas were revolutionary not just for mathematics but for the understanding of cognition and intelligence. He was among the first to ask whether machines could think, laying the groundwork for artificial intelligence. His Turing Test, a measure of a machine's ability to exhibit intelligent behavior indistinguishable from that of a human, remains a significant concept in both AI and philosophy.

  

Many now argue, with the introduction of capable large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT that Turing’s test has been overcome, as many now have difficulty distinguishing between an output generated by AI and a human. Not convinced? Try it yourself [here](https://ai-or-human.github.io/).

  

Understanding Turing is like holding a key to the intellectual treasury of computer science and artificial intelligence. His work has influenced not just how we think about machines but also how we conceptualize intelligence and consciousness. >

  

George Boole published "The Mathematical Analysis of Logic" in 1847 and "An Investigation of the Laws of Thought" in 1854. These works laid the foundation for Boolean algebra, which became fundamental in the development of digital circuit design and computer science.

  

Then came the era of 'Symbolic AI,' where researchers like John McCarthy and Marvin Minsky took these mathematical concepts and applied them to create the first AI programs. They used symbols to represent objects and ideas, creating rules and logic to manipulate them. This was the birth of AI as we know it—a field not just inspired by human intelligence but modeled on it.

  

So, why is this chapter of history crucial? Because it's the bridge between philosophy and application. Understanding these mathematical and computational roots will give you a deeper appreciation for the complex algorithms and technologies you'll encounter later in this course.

#### Early AI Programs 

Now that you know the mathematical and computational groundwork, let's jump into the early days of AI programming. This era was marked by a blend of groundbreaking innovation and, frankly, a bit of overconfidence.

  

The Summer of AI: Dartmouth Workshop, 1956

Imagine it's the summer of 1956, and a group of brilliant minds, including John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon - the early pioneers of AI - gather at Dartmouth College. They propose to spend the summer building a machine that could mimic human intelligence. You heard that right—a summer project to solve Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)! While their ambition was commendable, they vastly underestimated the complexity of human intelligence. The summer ended, but AGI remained an unsolved puzzle, marking the beginning of a series of ups and downs in AI research.

ELIZA and SHRDLU: The Pioneers

Despite the setbacks, the late 1950s and 1960s saw the development of some of the first AI programs. ELIZA, created by Joseph Weizenbaum, was a natural language processing program that could perform the role of a Rogerian psychotherapist. Then came SHRDLU, developed by Terry Winograd, which could understand and respond to queries about a world of geometric shapes. These programs were groundbreaking but also highlighted the limitations of early AI—they were good at specific tasks but far from the general intelligence the Dartmouth group had envisioned.

  

<SIDEBAR:

The Rogerian Psychotherapist

  

ELIZA was designed to mimic a Rogerian psychotherapist, a type of therapy where the therapist encourages the patient to talk more about their feelings and thoughts. The program didn't understand the conversation in the way humans do but used pattern matching and substitution techniques to generate responses.

  

Script-Driven

ELIZA operated based on a script, called DOCTOR, which contained a set of rules for generating responses. When a user inputted a sentence, ELIZA would scan the text for keywords like "I am," "my," or "feeling." Based on these keywords, ELIZA would generate a pre-defined but appropriately structured response, such as "Tell me more about your feelings."

[https://www.masswerk.at/elizabot/](https://www.masswerk.at/elizabot/) 

  

Limitations

  

While ELIZA was groundbreaking for its time, it had significant limitations. The program didn't understand the context or the emotional nuances of the conversation. It could not remember past interactions, making it incapable of providing a coherent and context-aware conversation over an extended period.

  

Why is ELIZA Important?

  

ELIZA was among the first programs to demonstrate that a machine could carry out a text-based conversation with a human, a significant milestone in the history of AI. It opened the door for further research into natural language processing and machine-human interaction, even if it was far from achieving true understanding or empathy.>

  

The Reality Check

The initial enthusiasm led to high expectations, but the limitations of these early programs became apparent. They were excellent at narrow tasks but lacked the ability to generalize their knowledge to broader contexts. This realization led to the first "AI winter," a period of reduced funding and interest in AI research, as the grand vision of AGI seemed increasingly elusive.

  

So, why is this part of history important? Because it teaches us humility and the value of incremental progress. The field of AI has always been a blend of grand visions and practical problem-solving. Understanding this history prepares you for the rollercoaster of innovations, setbacks, and renewed efforts that characterize AI to this day.

#### Machine Learning and Neural Networks

### VIDEO 4 - Explaining the perceptron 

As we move forward in time, we arrive at a pivotal moment in AI history—the advent of machine learning and neural networks. This era marked a shift from rule-based systems to algorithms that could learn from data, a development that brought us closer to the dream of creating machines that could "think."

  

The Perceptron: The Dawn of Learning Algorithms

In the late 1950s, Frank Rosenblatt introduced the Perceptron, an algorithm inspired by the structure of the human brain. The Perceptron could learn to classify simple patterns, and it was one of the first algorithms that allowed a machine to learn from its environment. However, it had limitations; it could only solve linearly separable problems, which are far simpler than most real-world tasks. 

  

An example of such a problem is to imagine you have a basket of apples and oranges, and you want to separate them. If all the apples are red and all the oranges are orange, you could easily draw a "line" (in this case, a mental boundary) to separate the two types of fruit based on their color. This is a linearly separable problem because you can use a single, straight line to separate the two classes.

  

In the context of machine learning, the Perceptron algorithm could easily solve this problem by learning the equation for the "line" that separates the apples from the oranges. However, if the problem were more complex—say, you had to separate fruits based on both color and weight—then a straight line might not suffice.

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/58_zEKo2WkPBpeRSOn-5TtTwO-7JWU7RkUdCUqKOO9JRrjnqJbfDd-jr2viEU1zEkAdXTBOIVJ5D_sqStBmVCJdrgiT0OOuqDQls5VthTvba4GQg4OD11ptCm21MK7RVfZDsVh6GKOMbaLD9dpYOk1Q)

  

Backpropagation: Learning to Learn

Fast forward to the 60s and 70s, and we encounter the concept of backpropagation, a technique that allows neural networks to adjust their internal parameters based on the error of their predictions. It wasn’t until 1989, though, that the technique was popularized by David E. Rumelhart, Geoffrey E. Hinton and Ronald J. William in the paper [Learning representations by back-propagating errors](https://www.nature.com/articles/323533a0).

  

Imagine you're teaching a dog to fetch a ball. You throw the ball, and the dog goes after it. If the dog successfully fetches the ball, you give it a treat. If not, you might show the dog what you expect by guiding it to the ball. This is a loop of action, error-checking, and correction.

  

Backpropagation works similarly but in a mathematical way. When a neural network makes a prediction, it compares the prediction to the correct answer to measure the "error." It then goes back through the network in reverse to adjust its internal settings, aiming to reduce the error next time it makes a prediction.

  

While the underlying math, which involves calculus, is complex, the concept is straightforward: determine the error by assessing the accuracy of the prediction, and then distribute that "knowledge" back through the network to improve future predictions.

  

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/1HC7poqOpiD-wMIBlwqSTGbKwuPgqMFiAvEKP_7N2X0puhqR-XzNeVLNmASlQbKcVRZruV0Demq5IOggrxI1XY_76-XeWwZfMJAUnIJNWjGsHfvbXwBgZF3tchxgvolFbi__Udkht_LmC5fevd-JkjM)

  

This was a game-changer. Suddenly, machines could learn complex functions and generalize from examples, opening the door to applications like image recognition, natural language processing, and more.

  

#### The Deep Learning Revolution

The term "deep learning" came into prominence in the 2000s, but its roots can be traced back to earlier neural network research. The breakthrough came when researchers like Geoffrey Hinton, Yann LeCun, and Yoshua Bengio started using multiple layers in neural networks, coupled with new machine learning training techniques and faster hardware. This allowed the networks to learn more complex representations of data, leading to the term "deep" learning to signify the use of many layers.

  

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/BazW3ui_Xiuz8AZKyA3jeDjFzc0wviRNIohfxSIV5mXXzALz6PbXtcI933ofTJq_HSINav-xQ6dtViwasd4Y7J42eAtnlrHQk9YwHjBdfai_tuLW6pk4jPT5BoxLtXT3FPAha6O9o3pOd-8zvhVy3oU)

Starting second from left, Yann LeCun, Geoffrey Hinton, and Yoshua Bengio. 

[https://sh-tsang.medium.com/deep-learning-for-ai-by-yoshua-bengio-yann-lecun-and-geoffrey-hinton-23c16a479300](https://sh-tsang.medium.com/deep-learning-for-ai-by-yoshua-bengio-yann-lecun-and-geoffrey-hinton-23c16a479300)

  

In a neural network, a layer is essentially a set of mathematical operations that transform the input data in some way. Imagine a car factory assembly line where each station (layer) adds something new or modifies the product (data) as it passes through. At the first station we get the car’s frame, then parts of the interior such as the seats and engine become pieces in the product. Another station adds the exterior mirrors, another the panels, and so on… At each station/step we are adding a layer of complexity and function that relies on the other parts (data) to get a fully functional car. In a deep learning model, the "deep" refers to having multiple such stations or layers.

  

Input Layer: This is where the network receives its initial data, like pixels in an image.00000

  

Hidden Layers: These are the layers in between the input and output. Each hidden layer learns to extract increasingly abstract features from the input. For example, in image recognition, the first layer might learn to recognize edges, the next layer shapes, and even further layers could recognize complex structures like faces or animals.

  

Output Layer: This is where the final prediction or classification is made.

  

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/UcuRTv6oUj2XkpfldZd3XOao4E7obHJXa57fiKyZS9MS_5dqREqRB54XY99--tiZq0KBuZpddmRWt_lFCmj5vuHDr8AZjJkrrXGh3za5x17lb3Hp-vfpxjCdNSWLY5m3qxl7PwTV929aczba4BaRDOI)

  

The magic of deep learning lies in these layers. As data passes through each layer, the network learns to recognize increasingly complex features. By the time the data reaches the output layer, the network has a nuanced understanding of what it's looking at, be it a sentence, an image, or any other form of data. This ability to learn hierarchical features is what makes deep learning so effective for a wide range of AI applications, from natural language processing to autonomous vehicles.

  

Deep learning has revolutionized numerous fields. It powers the voice assistants in our phones, enables real-time translation, and even assists in medical diagnoses. It's the closest we've come to mimicking the learning processes of the human brain, and it's continually pushing the boundaries of what machines can do.

  

The Reality Check… Again

  

While these advancements were groundbreaking, they also led to renewed hype and, inevitably, another reality check. Deep learning models require vast amounts of data and computational power, and they often act as "black boxes," making it challenging to understand their decisions. This has led to ongoing research in AI explainability and ethics, topics we'll explore later in this course.

  

So, why is this era crucial? Because it represents the current frontier of AI research and application. Understanding the rise and limitations of machine learning and neural networks prepares you for the state-of-the-art technologies and ethical considerations that are shaping the field today.

#### Current and Future Trends in AI

  

State-of-the-Art Models: Transformers and Beyond

  

Imagine a world where machines not only understand human language but can generate it too, creating everything from poems to research papers. This isn't science fiction; it's the reality we live in today, thanks to a groundbreaking innovation known as the transformer architecture.

  

Before transformers, machine learning models struggled with understanding the context in language. They could analyze words in isolation but had difficulty grasping the nuances that come from word placement, sentence structure, and thematic consistency. Enter transformers, which turned this limitation on its head.

  

At its core, the transformer architecture excels at handling sequences of data, whether that's a sentence, a paragraph, or an entire document. Unlike its predecessors, it doesn't process words one at a time in a linear fashion. Instead, it looks at all the words simultaneously, capturing the relationships between them, no matter how far apart they are in the text. This ability to consider the 'global context' was a game-changer.

### VIDEO 5 - Transformers 

  

The secret sauce of transformers is something called 'attention mechanisms.' In simple terms, attention allows the model to focus on different parts of the input text differently. It's akin to how, when you're reading a complex article, your mind gives more weight to the sentences that help you understand the main argument. This selective focus enables transformers to capture nuances and relationships in the data that were previously elusive.

  

But why was this such a revolutionary leap? The answer lies in the model's unparalleled versatility and efficiency. Transformers can be trained on a massive scale, learning from billions of sentences to understand and generate human-like text. This scalability led to the development of generative AI models, like GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer), which can write essays, summarize articles, answer questions, and even generate code.

  

Moreover, transformers opened the door to 'transfer learning,' a concept where a model trained on one task can be adapted for another with minimal additional training. This was a monumental advancement, as it meant that the vast knowledge the model gained could be applied across multiple domains, making it both powerful and cost-effective.

  

In summary, transformers have redefined what's possible in the realm of AI. They've given machines the ability to understand and generate human language in a way that's contextually rich, nuanced, and incredibly versatile. And in doing so, they've brought us one step closer to a future where AI is not just a tool but a collaborative partner in human progress.

AI Safety: An Emerging Priority

As AI systems become more capable, ensuring their safety and alignment with human values has become a pressing concern. Research in AI safety focuses on making sure that as AI systems execute tasks, they do so in a way that is beneficial and poses minimal risks to humans.

  

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/w3MiILYm_cntrWkO6UgwbGPrCM8tD0wTthYsWGIgY-zWrN1vBj4Ta_MKe8q1R9Eb-nhhRwz73MOkKYLSBfLPnf7hTZHCQPfMkks5fFVvTjTSWGXUBZOBD7qs7XjXokzVUZoJO2R0chQtsWMeAr4KBkw)

  

One promising approach in AI safety is Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning (CIRL), where the AI system learns to align its objectives with those of the human user by observing their actions and receiving feedback. There are other approaches, but as of now, there is no tried and true way to effectively align AI with human values and preferences reliably.

  

Ethics in AI encompasses a range of issues, including fairness, accountability, and transparency. As AI systems are increasingly used in decision-making processes that affect human lives, ensuring that these systems are ethical and unbiased has become a focal point of modern AI research. We will touch on all of these in more depth throughout the course, but it is top of mind for how best to use and regulate AI responsibly so it provides more value, and results in less harm.

  

Efforts are underway to make AI systems more transparent in their decision-making and to eliminate biases in AI algorithms, especially those that can lead to unfair or discriminatory outcomes. However, similar to alignment there is no current way to interpret how these deep neural networks are actually “deciding” on their outputs.

  

The future of AI is not just about technological advancements but also about its responsible and ethical application. Whether it's in healthcare, education, or social justice, AI has the potential to drive significant positive change, provided it is developed and used responsibly.

Imagine a scholarly librarian named Rachel. She's meticulous, organized, and operates by a strict set of rules. Think of a GPS navigation system. It follows a set of rules to get you from point A to point B, much like Rachel would guide you to a book. It doesn't invent new routes; it selects the best one from its programmed options.

### Artificial Intelligence Types

#### Artificial Narrow Intelligence: The Specialist

  

Meet Nava, a virtuoso violinist. She can play Bach, Vivaldi, and Paganini with unparalleled skill. When it comes to the violin, she's a genius, much like our librarian Rachel is a genius in her domain of books and organization. However, if you ask Nava to play the drums or compose a symphony, she'd be at a loss. Her expertise is incredibly focused.

  

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/_Qd1LsNtTdomHrVjQC0FY_KAQvWelcLgSyGOHbCps_e29OHfuhDwjkAHHKf8KAf-kj3TAj_wI0SNs316DlcBdmG2daXwLxx_5cijcDn34lQ6UQSQxBQRIqsMWKlkW4wujy1HFig5e4Q1Hcwa3eQGyLw)

  

Like Rachel, Nava excels in a specific domain. Rachel's domain is organizing and retrieving books, while Nava's is playing the violin. Both are examples of specialized intelligence, akin to Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI).

  

Some examples of real world ANI’s are:

- Spam Filters: These are excellent at sorting your emails into 'Spam' or 'Not Spam,' but you wouldn't use them to recommend a movie.
    
- Voice Assistants: Siri or Alexa can help you set reminders or tell you the weather, but they can't write a research paper for you.
    

  

ANI’s are like alien savants - they are beyond genius level in their domain, but are unable to generalize to other tasks. Just as Nava can't play other instruments, ANI systems can't perform tasks outside their specific domain.

  

They also cannot transfer learning across different domains. Nava can't apply her violin skills to drumming. Similarly, a spam filter can't use its email-sorting knowledge to recommend movies.

  

Lastly, ANI’s are highly dependent on the data they are trained on. Nava needs sheet music, just as ANI systems need data specific to their task. Without it, their performance suffers.

  

In summary, Artificial Narrow Intelligence, represented by our virtuoso violinist Nava, excels in specialized tasks but lacks the versatility to go beyond its domain. Understanding this limitation helps us appreciate why we often need different types of AI for different tasks. It’s similar to calling specialists or experts for different needs that you have.

Meet Samuel, a modern day Renaissance Man and polymath. He's not just a painter; he's also a musician, a mathematician, a philosopher, and even a decent chef. Unlike Nava, who is a virtuoso in one domain, Samuel has a broad range of skills and can adapt to new challenges with ease. He embodies the concept of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI).

There are no current real-world Examples of AGI, since it does not exist yet, but here are some examples of what they might look like.

  
  
  

1. Personal Assistant: Imagine a digital assistant that not only sets reminders and schedules your meetings, but also helps you write research papers, solves complex math problems, and even gives you cooking tips.

2. Healthcare Companion: An AI system that can diagnose diseases, recommend treatments, and also provide psychological counseling.

  

AGI will be highly skilled in multiple domains and capable of transferring knowledge from one field to another. The best example of General Intelligence are humans themselves! Samuel doesn't need a script or sheet music to create; he adapts to the situation. AGI, too, can adapt to new tasks, even those it wasn't specifically trained for.

  

In summary, Artificial General Intelligence, represented by our Renaissance man Samuel, offers the promise of broad, adaptable, and transferable skills. Understanding this helps us appreciate the potential future of AI, where systems could be as versatile as a well-rounded human being.

#### Classical AI - American Express Fraud Detection

  

In the financial industry, fraud is a constant and evolving threat. American Express, a global financial services company, processes millions of transactions every day. The challenge for them is to identify fraudulent activities among these transactions quickly and accurately. They need to sift through massive amounts of data to detect unusual patterns that could indicate fraud, all while minimizing false positives that could inconvenience legitimate customers.

  

To meet this challenge, American Express turned to Classical AI techniques, specifically machine learning algorithms. They developed a system that uses predictive analytics, pattern recognition, and network link analysis to analyze and interpret historical transaction data.

  

The system employs predictive models to forecast the likelihood of a transaction being fraudulent based on various factors such as transaction amount, location, and the type of merchant. These models are trained on historical data and are continually updated to adapt to new types of fraud.

  

The system also uses pattern recognition algorithms to identify unusual spending behaviors. For example, if a card is used in two different countries within a short time frame, the system flags it for further review.

  

To add another layer of security, American Express uses network link analysis to examine the relationships between different transactions. This helps in identifying organized fraud rings that might otherwise go unnoticed when analyzing transactions in isolation.

  

By leveraging Classical AI techniques, American Express has been able to significantly reduce fraud, thereby saving millions of dollars and enhancing customer trust. This case study serves as a compelling example of how Classical AI can be used responsibly and effectively to solve real-world challenges.

### Glossary of Terms

  

Artificial Intelligence (AI) - The simulation of human intelligence in machines that are programmed to think like humans and mimic their actions.

  

Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) - A hypothetical AI system that possesses general intelligence and can understand or learn any intellectual task that a human being can. It does not currently exist.

  

Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI) - AI systems that are focused on a single, narrow task and are unable to reason or apply knowledge outside their domain. Examples include virtual assistants, self-driving cars, etc.

  

Artificial Super Intelligence (ASI) - A theoretical, futuristic AI system that surpasses human-level general intelligence and has capabilities far beyond what humans can do. It does not currently exist.

  

Backpropagation - A technique used to train neural networks, involving propagating errors made during training back through the network to adjust weights and reduce errors.

  

Bias - Systematic errors in AI systems stemming from flawed training data, algorithms, or human prejudice. Can lead to prejudicial or unfair outcomes.

  

Classical AI - Rule-based systems focused on symbolic reasoning, logic and knowledge representation to solve problems. Not capable of learning and improving with data/experience.

  

Explainable AI - Making AI model decisions and reasoning processes understandable to humans, for purposes of trust, accountability and improvement.

  

Generative AI - AI systems capable of generating new content like text, images, video, and audio in a human-like manner. 

  

Machine Learning - Algorithms that can learn from data to make predictions or decisions without explicit programming.

  

Neural Networks - Computing systems modeled on the biological neural networks of animal brains. Good at pattern recognition.

  

Responsible AI - Ethical development and use of AI systems, focused on fairness, accountability, transparency, inclusivity, and avoiding harm.
