#### Text-to-Speech (TTS)

##### Phonetic Algorithms: The Building Blocks of Speech

Phonetic Algorithms serve as the first chair in our algorithmic orchestra, setting the tone for the entire performance. These algorithms focus on converting written text into a series of phonemes, the smallest units of sound in a language. By understanding the phonetic structure of words, computers can begin the process of generating speech that closely resembles human articulation.

  

How It Works

1. Text Parsing: The algorithm starts by breaking down the input text into individual words and then further into syllables.
    
2. Phoneme Identification: Each syllable is mapped to its corresponding phoneme based on predefined rules or dictionaries.
    
3. Stress and Intonation: The algorithm also identifies the stress patterns and intonation to make the speech sound more natural.
    

  

Understanding phonetics is crucial for any sound synthesis system. It serves as the basis for more complex algorithms and technologies, ensuring that the speech generated is intelligible and natural-sounding.

  

Real-World Applications

- Voice Assistants: Phonetic algorithms help voice assistants like Siri and Alexa understand and generate human-like speech.
    
- Language Learning Apps: These algorithms are used in apps that teach pronunciation, helping users learn how to articulate words correctly.
    

  

Phonetic Algorithms are the unsung heroes of sound synthesis, providing the foundational elements that make natural-sounding speech possible. As we move on to more advanced algorithms, you'll see how they build upon this essential groundwork.

##### Concatenative Synthesis: The Composer of Sound Bytes

  

If Phonetic Algorithms are the building blocks of speech, then Concatenative Synthesis is the composer that arranges these blocks into a melodious tune. This algorithm works by stitching together pre-recorded snippets of sound, known as "sound units," to generate speech. It's like composing a musical piece by arranging individual notes in a specific sequence.

  

How It Works

1. Sound Unit Database: The algorithm relies on a database of pre-recorded sound units, which can range from phonemes to entire syllables or words.
    
2. Unit Selection: Based on the input text, the algorithm selects the most appropriate sound units from the database.
    
3. Sequencing and Blending: The selected units are then sequenced and blended together to produce continuous, natural-sounding speech.
    

  

Concatenative Synthesis is particularly useful for generating high-quality, natural-sounding speech. It's often used in applications where the quality of speech output is a critical factor.

  

Real-World Applications

- Audiobooks: This algorithm is commonly used to generate the narrations for audiobooks, ensuring a lifelike listening experience.
    
- Customer Service Bots: Many automated customer service systems use Concatenative Synthesis to interact with users in a natural, human-like manner.
    

  

Concatenative Synthesis serves as the composer in our algorithmic orchestra, skillfully arranging sound units to create speech that is both natural and engaging. As we delve deeper into other algorithms, you'll see how each plays a unique role in the symphony of sound synthesis.

##### Formant Synthesis: The Acoustic Sculptor

  

Formant Synthesis is the acoustic sculptor of our algorithmic ensemble, molding raw sound into the intricate shapes of human speech. Unlike Concatenative Synthesis, which uses pre-recorded sound units, Formant Synthesis generates speech by modeling the human vocal tract, creating a more flexible and dynamic system.

  

How It Works

1. Vocal Tract Modeling: The algorithm uses mathematical models to simulate the behavior of the human vocal tract.
    
2. Formant Frequencies: It identifies the formant frequencies, which are the resonant frequencies that give each vowel its unique sound.
    
3. Sound Generation: Using these models and frequencies, the algorithm generates speech by shaping the sound waves accordingly.
    

  

Formant Synthesis offers a high level of flexibility, allowing for more nuanced and expressive speech. It's particularly useful in applications where the text-to-speech needs to convey emotions or varied intonations.

  

Real-World Applications

- Speech Therapy Tools: Formant Synthesis is often used in tools designed to help individuals with speech impairments.
    
- Virtual Performers: This algorithm can be used to create virtual singers or narrators capable of expressive, emotive speech.
    

  

Formant Synthesis is like the acoustic sculptor of our algorithmic world, capable of molding sound into intricate forms that mimic the complexities of human speech, even allowing us to make AI (literally) sing.

  

##### Parametric TTS: The Machine Learning Maestro

Parametric Text-to-Speech (TTS) is the machine learning maestro of our algorithmic orchestra. Unlike traditional methods that rely on pre-recorded sound units or vocal tract modeling, Parametric TTS employs machine learning techniques to generate speech. It not only conducts the orchestra, but also learns and adapts over time.

  

How It Works

1. Data Training: The algorithm is trained on a large dataset of human speech, learning the nuances of intonation, stress, and rhythm.

2. Feature Extraction: It identifies key features from the input text, such as pitch, duration, and phonetic details.

3. Speech Synthesis: Using the trained model and extracted features, the algorithm generates speech that is both natural and dynamic.

  

Parametric TTS represents the cutting edge of speech synthesis, offering unparalleled flexibility and quality. Its machine learning capabilities allow it to adapt and improve, making it ideal for applications that require highly natural and expressive speech.

  

Real-World Applications

- Personal Assistants: Advanced voice assistants like Google Assistant and Siri often use Parametric TTS for more natural interactions.
    
- Accessibility: This algorithm can be used to create highly expressive and natural-sounding speech for individuals with speech impairments.
    

  

Parametric TTS is the future-facing maestro of our algorithmic orchestra, leveraging machine learning to create speech that is not just natural but also continually improving. As we wrap up our exploration of TTS algorithms, you'll see how each contributes to the rich and evolving landscape of sound synthesis.

#### Speech-to-Text: Transcribing Sounds

In the grand performance of sound synthesis, the algorithms we've explored so far focus on the production of speech - like musicians bringing a symphony to life. But what happens when we want to capture those sounds in a format machines can interpret, just as musical notes can be transcribed into sheet music? This is where Speech-to-Text (STT) algorithms come in. 

  

STT algorithms serve as the transcribers - converting spoken language into text that computers can understand. Instead of generating speech, they listen to speech and transcribe it. This transcription process enables seamless communication between humans and machines.

  

In this section, we'll explore the different components that work together to transcribe in STT. You'll see how acoustic modeling analyzes sound patterns, language modeling predicts word sequences, and decoding stitches these elements together.

  

By the end of this section, you'll understand how STT algorithms empower machines to achieve speech recognition, just as musical transcription preserves compositions for future performances. Let's dive in!

##### Acoustic Modeling: Analyzing the Sounds

If transcribing speech is like notating music, then acoustic modeling is the step where algorithms listen closely to identify the notes and rhythms. This component of STT algorithms focuses on analyzing the acoustic properties of speech to extract distinctive audio features. 

  

Here's an overview of how acoustic modeling works:

  

1. Audio Input: The algorithm takes an audio clip of speech as input. 
    
2. Feature Extraction: It analyzes the clip to identify acoustic features like pitch, intensity, resonance, etc. Popular techniques include MFCCs, filter banks, and spectrogram analysis.
    
3. Acoustic Models: The algorithm has been trained on many speech samples to create statistical models of different phonemes and sounds.
    
4. Matching Features: It compares the extracted audio features from the input clip to these models to identify speech components.
    

  

Real-World Examples:

  

- A voice assistant analyzes your spoken question to extract pitch, timing, and phonetic features. It matches these to acoustic models to recognize the words.
    
- Speech transcription software might analyze a long audio file, like a lecture recording, to identify distinct speech segments and phonetic components. 
    

  

The end result of acoustic modeling is effectively a set of distinguishable sound features that can then be correlated to phonetic and textual representations. This is the first step in transcribing speech into text.

  
  

##### Language Modeling: Predicting the Words

  

If acoustic modeling picks out the sounds, language modeling is about connecting them into coherent words and phrases. This component uses statistical language models to analyze speech patterns and predict the most likely sequences of words.

  

Here's how language modeling works in STT algorithms:

1. Corpus Analysis: The algorithm is trained on large bodies of textual data to build statistical language models. This helps it understand syntax and semantics.
    
2. Sequence Prediction: As acoustic modeling identifies phonetic components, language modeling predicts the most probable word sequences that fit those sounds. 
    
3. Improving Accuracy: Large, domain-specific corpora improve accuracy as the model learns nuances like vocabulary and grammar.
    

  

Real-World Examples:

- A medical STT tool would be trained on medical textbooks and journals to predict medical terminology spoken words. 
    
- Voice assistants use general language models but also learn user-specific patterns like names and colloquial terms.
    

  

We will go deep into the neural magic of Large Language Models in our Natural Language Processing section, but in the context of STT, these models  complement acoustic analysis to transcribe speech into coherent, accurate text. Next, we'll see how decoding brings everything together.

  

##### Decoding: Stitching It Together 

  

In our speech transcription orchestra, acoustic modeling analyzes sounds and language modeling predicts word patterns. Decoding is the component that brings everything together to translate speech into text. You will remember our overview of Decoding from the previous module - turning the math back into your chosen modality within a deep matrix (vector space) of weighted possibilities for output.

  

Here is a brief overview of how decoding works:

  

1. Combining Models: The acoustic models and language models are combined to create a knowledge graph of sounds, possible words, and transition probabilities.
    
2. Searching for Matches: As new speech input is fed, the decoder searches this graph to find the most likely text match.
    
3. Refining the Output: Techniques like beam search narrow down the options to the optimal transcription.
    

  

Real-World Examples:

- During a voice search query, decoding quickly interprets the speech based on audio features and word probabilities.
    
- Speech recognition apps use decoding to continuously translate speech into text in real-time.
    

  

While acoustic and language modeling extract patterns, decoding is what completes the puzzle. It enables machines to dynamically convert speech into text for seamless human-computer interaction.

  

##### Inside a Voice Assistant: A Speech-to-Text Journey

  

Let’s put it all together by walking through what happens when you, "Hey Siri, what's the weather today?"

  

1. Sound Input: Your spoken words enter the microphone as an analog audio signal. This gets digitized into a digital waveform.
    
2. Acoustic Modeling: Algorithms analyze the waveform, extracting features like MFCCs, pitch, timing, and phonetic qualities. These are matched with acoustic models to identify phonemes. 
    
3. Language Modeling: Based on the phonemes and acoustic patterns, statistical language models predict your words and phrases. The assistant's vocabulary and contextual understanding is used to refine the predictions. 
    
4. Decoding: Leveraging both the acoustic and language models, the decoder determines the most likely text match - "what's the weather today". 
    
5. Response: With your speech converted to understandable text, the assistant can process your meaning and provide a relevant voice response.
    

  

This behind-the-scenes look shows how seamlessly STT algorithms work together to enable voice-powered assistants. Next we'll wrap up with some interactive STT explorations.

  

#### The Power of Transcription

  

In this exploration of Speech-to-Text algorithms, we've learned how machines can listen to the music of speech and transcribe it into meaningful text. 

  

Acoustic modeling analyzes the component sounds. Language modeling connects them into words and phrases. Decoding stitches these elements together to find the most likely text match. Real-world applications like voice assistants demonstrate these algorithms in action.

  

Some key takeaways:

  

- STT enables seamless speech-based interaction by converting speech to machine-understandable text.
    
- Different modeling techniques analyze sounds, predict word patterns, and combine them for transcription.
    
- Large datasets and machine learning improve the accuracy and robustness of STT. 
    
- Applications like virtual assistants, dictation software, and voice search rely on STT.
    

  

STT opens up tremendous possibilities for convenient, natural language interfaces. As these algorithms continue advancing, so will the potential for machines to achieve human-level speech comprehension - a key step in advancing natural language processing.

### Generative Adversarial Network

The early 1980s was an era when computing power was rapidly increasing, setting the stage for new AI capabilities. It was in this environment that computer scientist Ian Goodfellow took an interest in “adversarial” algorithms - where two opposing AIs compete against each other to become more skilled. 

  

After the initial excitement and hype surrounding early AI research in the 1960s and 70s, progress slowed from the late 1980s through the 2000s, a period often called the "AI winter."

  

Funding and interest in AI declined due to unrealistic expectations and limitations of the technology at the time. Computers simply didn't have the raw processing power required for more advanced techniques like deep neural networks.

  

It wasn't until the 2000s and 2010s that a resurgence occurred. Rapid increases in computational capabilities, paired with vast datasets and improvements in machine learning algorithms fueled this AI renaissance.

  

So it wasn’t until 2014, 30+ years later, that Goodfellow formally introduced this adversarial framework for the hot field of deep learning, calling it generative adversarial networks or GANs. The approach was revolutionary - one AI generator network created content while a discriminator network tried to detect if it was real or fake. The two networks would evolve together through this counterfeiting game.

  

When applied to images, GANs could generate highly realistic pictures. The generator model learned to create images that fool the discriminator. This adversarial back-and-forth resulted in novel images indistinguishable from real photos.

<SIDEBAR: What is transfer learning? 

  

Transfer learning is like getting trained as a painter by mastering how to accurately recreate many existing paintings. Those fundamental skills can then be transferred to creating your own original paintings more easily, without having to learn to paint from scratch.

  

In AI, it involves taking a model already trained on image classification (identifying what's in an image) and using that as the starting point for training a new model on image generation. 

  

The classification model provides a strong visual understanding foundation before fine-tuning the new model on generating images based on text captions. It's akin to the painter learning techniques, color theory, brushwork etc. from copying classics, which informs their own work.

  

This transfer of knowledge removes the need for massive datasets to train capable generative models. The pretrained model gives it a major head start. The concept of transfer learning is a versatile one, and has greatly sped up advancement in all forms of Generative AI.

## Under the Hood

Modern text-to-image generation relies on a synthesis of multiple sophisticated AI techniques:

  

- Neural networks - The backbone of deep learning models that can learn complex mappings from inputs to outputs. Remember the Convolutional Neural Networks analyze visual patterns in images.
    
- Generative adversarial networks (GANs) - The innovative type of neural network architecture where two models compete against each other to become better at generation and discrimination.

- Diffusion models - Cutting-edge generative models that can synthesize highly realistic images through a denoising process.
    

  

These key components work together to enable AI systems to generate photographic images from text captions. Neural networks provide the computational framework, GANs and VAEs help map between visual and textual domains, and diffusion models produce the realistic image outputs.

### Image Datasets

Training sophisticated text-to-image models requires massive datasets of images paired with captions or descriptions. Here are some key image datasets used:

  

- ImageNet:One of the earliest large-scale image datasets with over 14 million categorized images, widely used to pretrain models. 
    
- COCO:The Common Objects in Context dataset with over 200,000 labeled images focused on object recognition.
    
- Visual Genome: A dense image annotation dataset with over 108,000 images described using structured scene graphs.
    
- LAION: A huge web-scraped open dataset from CommonCrawl containing over 5 billion image-text pairs. 
    
- Conceptual Captions: Higher quality dataset with 3.3 million crowd-sourced captions describing images in greater conceptual detail.
    
- CIFAR-10 - Collection of 60,000 32x32 images across 10 classes like airplanes, cars, birds, used to benchmark computer vision models.
    

  

Larger datasets support training more capable models but can perpetuate societal biases if not properly curated for diversity and representation. Strategic dataset mixing and augmentation is key for mitigating limitations while providing sufficient data. Many of the newer methods more actively curate the datasets to train models on more high-quality or narrowly focused captions and image pairs.

Text Encoders 

  

As you'll recall from our previous module introducing encodings, text needs to be converted into vectors before models can process it. Now we'll explore how text encoders work specifically for guiding text-to-image generation.

  

The model maps encoded text vectors to latent image vectors, providing control over image features. Methods like word embeddings and CLIP encode text into a semantic form the model understands. Further fine-tuning of text encoders can also reduce embedding bias and improve coherence. 

  

Remember that these encoders are translators that convert words into a language the AI system can comprehend. Just as human languages have different vocabularies and grammars, machines have their own language too based on probabilities. 

  

If you will recall, word embeddings are like a dictionary, mapping each word into its closest machine-language equivalent based on learned semantic meanings from previous training. This statistical web becomes the vocabulary understood by the model.

  

CLIP encoders act as an interpreter of sorts, training the machine how to grasp visual concepts described in text captions. It aligns the visual and textual domains into a shared representation.

  

Prompt engineering formats text in a strategic syntax tailored to the model’s patterns of “thinking”. So imagine someone who speaks Spanish and English, and someone who only speaks Chinese, and a final person in the middle who speaks English and Chinese. You can arrange the conversation so everything is translated properly.

  

In the same way, the text encoding is transformed into its language, the AI is ready to translate descriptions into image vectors and generate corresponding visuals. Text encoders enable crossing the language barrier between human and machine.

Overall imagine the path of a text-to-image journey to be one of moving between blurred noise, and clear imagery in through many iterations back and forth.

  

Forward Diffusion

The path of diffusion starts with a real image and progressively adds noise to it through multiple steps. At each step, a small amount of randomness is introduced that obscures the original image slightly

  

Over many iterations, enough noise accumulates that the result becomes completely unrecognizable and decoupled from the initial image. At the end, we're left with pure noise like static on an old TV screen.

Reverse Diffusion

  

Reverse diffusion goes in the opposite direction - taking pure noise and slowly removing randomness to reveal a real image. It progresses in reverse through the forward diffusion process, removing a bit of noise at each step to incrementally denoise the image.

  

Given the conditioning path of the forward pass, the reverse pass can reconstruct the original image from noise by undoing the additions step-by-step. Text encodings, a prompt, provide guidance during reverse diffusion, steering the denoising process to produce an image reflecting the description.

  

The Denoising Process

  

The core of reverse diffusion is the denoising function that removes noise from the image at each step. This is what guides the sampling process to reconstruct the original image from random noise. 

  

A common architecture used is the U-Net which has shown strong performance for denoising tasks. It contains:

  

1. Encoder - Maps the input image into a lower-dimensional latent space through a series of downsampling layers. In other words it captures higher-level features like lines, edges, etc.
    
2. Decoder - Gradually up samples the latent representation back into the original image shape through a series of upsampling layers. So, just like you look at two dots and a line and you’ll imagine a face, it begins re-constructing more complicated patterns as it passes through each layer, and the neurons either fire or don’t, in part influenced by the prompt.
    
3. Skip Connections - Pass encodings from the encoder directly to the decoder at the same level, which helps preserve finer details with each de-noising pass.
    
4. Residual Connections - Element-wise additions combine decoder outputs with outputs from previous layers, which allows gradients and clearer images.
    

  

At each denoising step, the U-Net takes a noisy image as input, passes it through the encoder-decoder framework, and outputs a less noisy version. Text encodings are incorporated through cross-attention layers. 

  

A cross attention layer is a type of attention mechanism like we discussed in the previous module. It uses a neural networks that allows interaction between elements from different sources, such as between an image and text. 

  

You will remember the “query”, “keys” and “values” are means to properly weigh tokens (parts of a word) in a sentence. This is only done across one modality - text in this case. In a cross attention layer, the "query" comes from one modality (e.g. encoded text) and the "keys" and "values" come from another modality (e.g. image features). In the case of diffusion, attention weights are calculated between the text and image elements.

  

This allows the model to learn alignments between representations from different modalities. In text-to-image generation, cross attention lets the model dynamically relate the text encodings to different spatial regions in the image during the diffusion process.

  

The text encodings can guide the image generation by biasing the model to emphasize certain visual features through this cross-modality attention. This helps improve coherence between the text description and generated image.

  

This conditions the model to steer outputs toward images reflecting the provided prompt.

### Training and Fine-Tuning

As we have discussed in previous modules, creating a capable model, including text-to-image, requires extensive training on large datasets. The model learns to generate plausible images matching text descriptions through this training process. 

  

There are two primary training paradigms: 1) Training from scratch by initializing random weights and learning end-to-end on a massive dataset 2) Fine-tuning by starting from a pretrained model and adapting it to perform better on a smaller custom dataset.

  

Training from scratch demands extraordinary data and computation but allows complete flexibility. Fine-tuning enables efficient customizing for new domains by updating only certain weights. 

  

Here is an overview of training text-to-image models from scratch:

  

Training from Scratch

  

Training a text-to-image model from scratch involves randomly initializing the model weights and learning directly on a massive dataset:

  

- Data at Scale - Requires millions to billions of image-text pairs to learn associations.
    
- Random Weight Initialization - Model starts with randomly assigned connection weights.
    
- End-to-End Training - All model components trained jointly on a dataset through backpropagation.
    
- Extensive Compute - Training can take thousands of GPU/TPU hours due to model size. 
    
- Overfitting Risk - Prone to overfitting without sufficient data volume and diversity. 
    
- Flexible Capabilities - Allows customizing architecture and objectives without constraints.
    

  

While training from scratch demands extraordinary resources, it provides complete flexibility to tailor the model architecture and learning objectives. This approach powered DALL-E and other leading text-to-image models to date.

  

Fine-Tuning Text-to-Image Models

  

Fine-tuning starts with a pretrained text-to-image model and updates it for a specific domain or use case:

  

- Pretrained Initialization - Start with weights from a model trained on a massive general dataset. 
    
- Smaller Dataset - Fine-tune on a smaller, domain-specific dataset.
    
- Partial Weight Updates - Only modify certain layers and connections.
    
- More Efficient - Requires less data and compute than full training.
    
- Retains Knowledge - Avoids forgetting broadly useful patterns.
    
- Customization - Can specialize for new styles, concepts, objectives. 
    
- Careful Regularization - Important to avoid overfitting to limited data.
    

  

Fine-tuning makes training high-quality text-to-image models accessible with reasonable resources.

  

#### Fine-Tuning Techniques

There are three primary methods for fine-tuning a pre-trained neural network: Style Embeddings, Reinforcement Learning, and control networks.

  

Style Embeddings

  

Style embeddings offer an efficient way to make a text-to-image model mimic specific artistic styles, without retraining the whole model.

  

The idea is to train an extra small neural network, called an embedding, that links a style name like "cubist" to the visual patterns in a dataset of cubist artworks.

  

Then when generating images, the model can be prompted to produce outputs "in a cubist style" by referring to that embedding. The text cue activates the cubist patterns.

  

This way, a model can be adapted to many different styles by training lightweight embeddings on much smaller datasets than full model training would need.

  

For instance, new embeddings could customize the model to generate Impressionist, Abstract, or Pop Art styles when prompted with those keywords. The core model remains intact.

  

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/EHF9CmSPWWCRudQg85DpduITWUOZIxwpzXqVtBNdujS0BcelaKPJOMXNwwPyiFxIEecbQx9tRLbZGqwjEH4LAxiOC5NsV9bhlXd6w6eTOgiUb-eRcPGgkUTmk9cWWzU8DgkjBqgIRwC4sJjOBxsFuWQ)

  

Style embeddings allow fine-tuning a text-to-image model to mimic particular artistic styles:

  

- Style Dataset - Collect images exemplifying the desired visual style.
    
- Embed Artistic Style - Use the dataset to train an embedding linking the style name to image features.
    
- Textual Guidance - Prompt the model to generate images "in the style of" the style embedding.
    
- Visually Consistent - Images produced will reflect the artistic style learned from the dataset.
    
- Custom Stylization - Can create embeddings for a wide range of styles.
    
- Compact and Efficient - Embeddings are relatively small and fast to train. 
    

  

Style embeddings enable end users to adapt general text-to-image models to produce outputs matching specific visual styles. This provides a powerful artistic tool.

  

Reinforcement Learning

  

Reinforcement learning gives a way to fine-tune text-to-image models using interactive human feedback as a training signal. The key idea is to define a reward function that scores model outputs. For example, assigning higher rewards to more realistic or creative images.

  

As the model generates images, it receives these rewards as feedback to shift its behavior towards more highly rewarded outputs. For instance, having humans score generated images from 1-10 on realism trains the model to achieve higher average scores through its internal iterations.

  

Reinforcement learning allows interactively shaping text-to-image models based on human evaluation of results, beyond just datasets through:

  

- Defining Reward Function - Specify a reward to maximize, e.g. realism or coherence.
    
- Policy Optimization - Train model actions to maximize cumulative reward.
    
- Interactive Rewards - Humans can provide interactive feedback as rewards. This is called Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback.
    
- Maximizing Rewards - Fine-tunes model to specialize in highly rewarded outputs. 
    
- Custom Objectives - Wide range of rewards can be optimized, e.g. creativity.
    
- Challenging Setup - Careful design needed to define effective rewards.
    

  

Reinforcement learning empowers using custom rewards to shape model behavior during text-to-image generation. This expands creative possibilities.

  

Control Networks

Fine-tuning a text-to-image model using only a small dataset can mess up what it already knows. Control networks offer a safer way to add custom conditioning without breaking the pretrained model.

  

The idea is to make a copy of each part of the model. One copy stays frozen to preserve the original knowledge. The other copy gets trained on the small new dataset.

  

At generation time, the model blends together the outputs of the two copies based on the specific conditioning desired. This adds customization without interfering with the foundation.

  

For example, a model could be trained on just a few hundred images of flowers to specialize in flower generation, without losing its ability to also generate other objects it learned originally.

Control networks allow adapting text-to-image models for new conditioning data while protecting what's already been learned.

  

Control networks modularly incorporate conditioning into text-to-image models:

  
  

- Duplicate Model - Make two copies of each generative model component. 
    
- Lock Original - Freeze weights of one copy to preserve pretrained behavior.
    
- Train Control - Train the other copy on conditional data.
    
- Combine Paths - Use both model copies in generation, blending based on condition.
    
- Scalable Training - Enables training conditioning with limited data/compute. 
    
- Adaptable Control - Conditioning can be flexibly added or removed.
    
- Example Use - Train control model on small custom dataset to specialize output.

## Glossary of Terms

- Acoustic modeling: Analyzing the acoustic properties of speech in speech recognition systems.
    
- Attention mechanisms: Allow neural networks to focus on relevant parts of the input. Used in transformers. 
    
- Concatenative synthesis: Stitching together pre-recorded sound segments to generate speech.
    
- Decoding: Converting encoded representations back into desired outputs like text or images.
    
- Diffusion models: Generative models that produce images through progressive denoising.
    
- Fine-tuning: Adapting a pretrained model to perform better on a specific task.
    
- Formant synthesis: Generating speech by modeling the vocal tract to shape sound waves.
    
- Generative adversarial networks (GANs): Models where two neural networks compete against each other during training.
    
- Parametric synthesis: Using machine learning and neural networks for text-to-speech. 
    
- Reinforcement learning: Optimizing models through interactive human feedback rewards.
    
- Style embeddings: Enable mimicking artistic styles by linking text prompts to image patterns.
    
- Text encodings: Converting text into vector representations that models can process. 
    
- Transfer learning: Applying knowledge gained on one task to improve learning on a related task. 
    
- Transformers: Neural network architecture particularly adept at language tasks.
    
- Backpropagation: Method for calculating gradients to adjust neural network weights during training.
    
- Knowledge graph: Network linking related concepts and data to support reasoning.
    
- Language modeling: Predicting likely word sequences based on statistical patterns. 
    
- Neural networks: Computing systems modeled on the brain's neurons.
    
- Phonemes: The smallest distinguishable units of sound in a language. 
    
- Prompt engineering: Carefully formatting text prompts to steer model outputs.
    
- Regularization: Techniques to prevent overfitting during training.
    
- Variational autoencoders (VAEs): Neural networks adept at learning compressed data representations.
