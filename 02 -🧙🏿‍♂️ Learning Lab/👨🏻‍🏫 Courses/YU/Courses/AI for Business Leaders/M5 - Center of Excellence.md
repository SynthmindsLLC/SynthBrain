## 5.1 Criteria for Selection

The success of your Center of Excellence (CoE) largely depends on the people who are part of it. These "champions," are the driving force behind the CoE's initiatives, ensuring that projects align with organizational goals and values. They serve as the bridge between the CoE and the rest of the organization, facilitating communication, and ensuring that AI technologies are adopted responsibly and effectively.

  

Selecting the right champions for your Center of Excellence is a nuanced process that goes beyond technical expertise. Here are some key criteria to consider:

  

Technical Proficiency

While it's important that your champions have a solid understanding of AI and related technologies, this is just the starting point. They should be able to understand the technical intricacies and be conversant with the latest trends and advancements in the field. Therefore it’s important to allow a percentage of their time to experiment and read the latest information available on products and capacity.

  

Leadership Skills

Champions should be able to inspire and lead teams. They need to be adept at project management, capable of setting goals, and skilled at motivating team members to achieve them. The world of AI is ever-changing. Your champions should be adaptable and open to learning, ready to pivot when necessary and agile enough to adopt new technologies or methodologies.

  

Communication Skills

Effective communication is crucial. Your champions should be able to articulate complex technical concepts in a way that non-technical stakeholders can understand. They should also be skilled in conflict resolution and be able to facilitate discussions between different departments or teams. These soft skills are perhaps the most important, since this technology will be very new to most people in your organization, which can easily become a barrier to implementation. If no one is using the technology, then what’s the point?

  

Ethical Understanding

Given the ethical implications of AI, your champions should have a strong moral compass. They should understand the ethical considerations surrounding AI and be committed to responsible usage. They will serve as the models for adhering to the organizational policies.

  

Diversity

A diverse team brings in a range of perspectives, which is invaluable in problem-solving and decision-making. Look for champions who bring different skill sets, experiences, and viewpoints to the table.

  

Alignment with Organizational Goals

Last but not least, your champions should be in sync with the broader goals and values of your organization. Their work should contribute to the overall mission and be aligned with the AI values and policies you've established.

  

Depending on your organization’s capacity and resources, you may not have the luxury of finding staff who can meet all of these criteria. That’s fine, the most important criteria to consider are finding people who are curious, tech-savvy, and able to manage complex projects. This should be seen as a highly sought after position, and be compensated or otherwise recognized appropriately. The world is an open marketplace, and these will be employees you do not want to have to replace in a year. The employees who populate the center for excellence will be the engine of your organization, so you will want to ensure they are in it for the long run.

## 5.2 Choosing the Right Structure for Your Center of Excellence

### Video 2

The Center of Excellence (CoE) is not just a committee or a project team; it's a strategic organizational unit dedicated to excellence in a particular area—in this case, AI. Its role is multifaceted, encompassing governance, best practices, support, and innovation. The CoE is the linchpin that holds your AI strategy together, ensuring that it's not just a collection of disparate projects but a cohesive, well-coordinated initiative.

  

Choosing the right structure for your CoE is akin to laying the foundation for a building. The structure will dictate how information flows, how decisions are made, and how quickly your organization can adapt to changes in the AI landscape. It's a decision that has long-term implications and can be the difference between a CoE that thrives and one that merely survives.

  

The structure you choose will be influenced by various factors such as your organization's size, culture, existing operational model, and the specific challenges you aim to address with AI. There are three primary structures to consider: Centralized, Decentralized, and Hub-and-Spoke. Each has its unique advantages and challenges, and understanding these can help you make an informed decision.

  

In the following sections, we will delve into the specifics of each structure, providing you with the insights you need to make the best choice for your organization.

### Centralized

In a Centralized Center of Excellence (CoE) for AI, all AI specialists and resources are grouped into a single team that falls under one business unit. This structure allows for greater control over AI initiatives, facilitates easy collaboration within the team, and enables the organization to strategize across various departments for standardization and consistency.

  

Some Pros to consider for this structure include:

  

Control

In a centralized structure, all AI initiatives are overseen by a single team, usually within one business unit. This setup allows for a high degree of control over AI projects, ensuring that they align closely with the organization's overarching goals and values. It's akin to having a dedicated command center that directs all AI-related activities, ensuring that they are in sync with the company's strategic objectives.

  

Collaboration

When all AI specialists are part of one team, it naturally fosters an environment of internal collaboration. Team members can easily share knowledge, resources, and expertise, making it easier to tackle complex AI challenges. Think of it as a specialized task force where each member brings a unique skill set, and they can easily collaborate because they're all in the same room, both literally and metaphorically.

  

Standardization

A centralized structure is conducive to the enforcement of standards and best practices across the organization. With a single team responsible for all AI initiatives, it's easier to maintain consistency and quality in the projects. This is similar to having a quality control unit that ensures every product meets the set standards before it goes to market.

  

While a centralized structure offers many advantages, it also comes with the risk of creating a disconnect between the AI team and the end-users in other departments. Being part of a single business unit can sometimes lead to tunnel vision, where the AI solutions developed may not fully meet the diverse needs of the entire organization. It's like having an elite squad that's excellent at what they do but may be out of touch with the realities or challenges faced by other units in the organization.

Best Fit for Centralized Structure

  

Organizations that would benefit most from a centralized structure often have the following characteristics:

  

1. Early Stage of AI Adoption: If your organization is in the early stages of AI adoption, a centralized structure can help you build a strong foundation by focusing all expertise and resources in one place.
    
2. Highly Regulated Industries: In sectors like healthcare, finance, or government, where compliance and standardization are critical, a centralized approach can be beneficial for maintaining strict control.
    
3. Complex AI Initiatives: If your AI projects require a high level of specialized knowledge and coordination, centralizing the expertise can be more effective.
    
4. Limited Resources: Smaller organizations or those with limited resources can benefit from a centralized structure as it allows for more efficient use of available resources.
    
5. Need for Speed: If rapid deployment and scaling of AI initiatives are a priority, a centralized team can act more swiftly, but at the potential cost of buy-in.
    
6. Cultural Fit: Organizations with a culture that values centralized decision-making and has a history of successful centralized initiatives may find this structure more natural to adopt.
    

  
  
  

Think of it as choosing the right vehicle for your journey. If you're going off-road, you'll need a different type of vehicle than if you're driving in the city. The centralized structure is your SUV for navigating the complex terrains of AI in a controlled, coordinated manner.

### Decentralized

  

In a decentralized Center of Excellence (CoE) structure, AI expertise is woven into the fabric of the organization, spread across various business units or departments. Imagine each department having its own AI specialists, deeply embedded in the unit's day-to-day operations and objectives. This close proximity allows for AI solutions to be finely tailored to the unique needs of each department, offering a level of customization that's hard to achieve in a centralized setup.

  

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/DxmRtFPO-zvlMAqxGAnKju6bmw1uxtbKYKrJQXiRt_-QkqQF1cvS9tnsExLAXAKd2tJZFdhoQrqXAkCkTlyC8353KlArNd6fh0uPC_YZYIqJOrjNBR1j6RBUQVeHtIfxL1YBktoeeHErps7cCspeBXc)

  

Pro’s

The beauty of this structure lies in its flexibility. When a department faces a specific challenge or opportunity, it can act swiftly, adapting its AI tools and strategies without waiting for approval from a centralized authority. This agility can be a game-changer, especially in fast-paced industries where quick decision-making is crucial.

  

Con’s

However, the decentralized model is not without its drawbacks. One significant issue is the lack of standardization. When each department is left to its own devices, you might find that they each develop their own methods and standards. This can lead to inconsistencies across the organization, making it difficult to integrate solutions or share knowledge. 

  

Additionally, this structure can sometimes lead to resource duplication. It's not uncommon for different departments to develop similar AI solutions or use a variety of different tools independently of each other, which is clearly an inefficient use of resources. And let's not forget the potential for silos. The absence of a centralized AI team can result in less inter-departmental communication, leading to isolated pockets of expertise that don't benefit the organization as a whole.

  

Best Fit for Decentralized Structure

The decentralized structure is particularly well-suited for large, complex organizations with diverse business units that have distinct objectives and operational needs. If your organization is spread across different geographical locations, or if you operate in multiple sectors with varying regulatory requirements, this model offers the flexibility you need.

  

It's also a strong fit for companies that prioritize agility and quick decision-making. If you're in a fast-paced industry where conditions change rapidly—think tech startups, e-commerce, or digital marketing—a decentralized CoE allows individual units to adapt without being bogged down by bureaucracy.

  

However, this model requires a mature approach to AI and a high level of expertise spread throughout the organization. If your company is still in the early stages of its AI journey, or if you lack the resources to hire multiple in-house experts, then a decentralized structure might not be the best fit for you at this time.

### Hub and Spoke

In the Hub-and-Spoke model, you have a centralized team that serves as the "hub," responsible for strategy, governance, and best practices. This hub is complemented by "spokes," which are specialized AI teams embedded within various departments. This structure aims to combine the best of both worlds: the centralized control and standardization of the centralized model with the specialized, department-specific focus of the decentralized model.

  

Pros

- High Specialization and Productivity: The spokes can focus on department-specific projects, leading to high levels of specialization and productivity.
    
- Knowledge Sharing and Upskilling: The hub serves as a knowledge center, disseminating best practices and upskilling the spokes, thereby increasing AI knowledge and use across the organization.
    
- Consistency and Feedback Loop: The hub ensures consistency in AI practices while receiving direct feedback from the spokes, who are the actual users of the AI.
    
- Balance of Flexibility and Efficiency: This model offers a balance between the flexibility of decentralized teams and the efficiency of a centralized hub.
    
- Consultant-Friendly: Easier to bring in external consultants to fill temporary gaps in expertise, as they can be easily integrated into the hub or any of the spokes.
    

  

Cons

- Complex Coordination and Budgeting: Managing multiple spokes and a central hub can make coordination and budgeting more complex.
    

  

Best For

Organizations that are looking for a balanced approach would benefit from the Hub-and-Spoke model. It's particularly useful for larger organizations where departments have very different needs but also require some level of standardization and control. This model is also beneficial when you want to foster innovation and experimentation in individual departments while maintaining an overarching strategy and governance structure.

### Decision Factors for Choosing a CoE Structure

  

When it comes to selecting the most appropriate structure for your Center of Excellence, several factors come into play. These factors will help you align your CoE with your organization's goals, culture, and operational needs. Here are some key considerations:

  

Organizational Size

Larger organizations may benefit from a Hub-and-Spoke model to manage complexity, while smaller organizations might find a centralized structure more manageable.

  

Business Goals

What are you trying to achieve with AI? If it's about standardization across the board, a centralized model might be best. If it's about specialized applications in different departments, consider a decentralized or Hub-and-Spoke model.

  

Risk Tolerance

Organizations with a higher risk tolerance may opt for a decentralized model to encourage innovation. Those who are more risk-averse might prefer the control offered by a centralized model.

  

Resource Availability

Do you have the resources to manage a complex Hub-and-Spoke model, or would a simpler centralized model be more feasible?

  

Cultural Fit

Some organizations have a culture of centralization and strict governance, while others thrive on autonomy and decentralization. Choose a model that fits your organizational culture.

  

Speed of Implementation

If you need to get your CoE up and running quickly, a centralized model might be the quickest to implement. However, if you have the time and resources for a more thoughtful approach, a Hub-and-Spoke model could offer long-term benefits.

  

Expertise Level

The existing level of AI expertise across different departments can also influence your decision. A Hub-and-Spoke model can be beneficial if you have varying levels of expertise across departments, versus only a couple of people who have the knowledge and skills required to implement the technology.

  

Stakeholder Buy-in

Last but not least, consider the level of buy-in from key stakeholders. A model that has broad support will be easier to implement and more likely to succeed. It is important that you have at least developed and communicated your AI values prior to establishing the CoE, as it will have implications for your success.

### Step 1: Planning and Roadmap

The first step in implementing your Center of Excellence is to create a comprehensive plan and roadmap. This is the blueprint that will guide your CoE's development and operations. It outlines what you aim to achieve, how you plan to do it, and the timeline for each milestone. 

  

Define Objectives

Start by defining the objectives of your CoE. What are the key goals you want to achieve? These could range from driving AI adoption across departments to standardizing AI practices and ensuring ethical AI usage. Remember it’s important to start with PROBLEMS not SOLUTIONS when considering AI implementations.

  

Conduct a Gap Analysis

Perform a gap analysis to identify what resources you currently have and what you'll need. This includes skills, technologies, and data. The gap analysis will inform your resource allocation in the next step. This can be done in a variety of ways, such as organizational surveys, interviews, or focus groups. Consider the most common pain points of your organization, and solve for them through leveraging AI.

  

Project Management

If possible, it should be someone’s full time job to help coordinate the process to completion by

1. Creating a realistic timeline for each milestone: This should include short-term goals (3-6 months), medium-term goals (6-12 months), and long-term goals (1-3 years). 
    
2. Assigning Responsibilities: Clearly define who is responsible for what. This includes the CoE team, stakeholders, and any external consultants you might bring in. Make sure everyone knows their roles and responsibilities.
    
3. Accountability: Someone needs to be ensuring the trains are moving smoothly, and that progress is being made against the agreed upon goals.

### Step 2: Resource Allocation

Once you have a well-defined plan and roadmap, the next step is to allocate the necessary resources. This involves budgeting, manpower planning, and identifying other essential assets. Proper resource allocation ensures that your Center of Excellence has the tools and talent it needs to meet its objectives.

  

Budgeting

Determine the budget needed to achieve the milestones outlined in your roadmap. This should include costs for technology, training, and possibly external consultants. Make sure to also account for ongoing operational expenses.

  

Talent and Resource Planning

Identify the skill sets you'll need in your CoE team. Will you need data scientists, computer programmers, IT, or project managers? Once you know what you need, you can decide whether to train existing employees or hire new talent.

  

Infrastructure and Tools

What technical resources will your CoE require? This could range from software licenses to cloud storage solutions. Make sure these align with your organization's existing tech stack to ensure smooth integration. You likely have many of these tools available in some form already, so do a landscape or inventory on what is already accessible. There is a real danger of SaaS bloat, where you have so many tools, it becomes difficult to use any of them.

  

Risk Mitigation

Consider potential risks such as technology failure, skill gaps, or budget overruns. Develop contingency plans to address these risks should they arise.

### Step 3: KPIs and Metrics

Identifying the right KPIs is a critical step in the success of your Center of Excellence. These KPIs should align closely with the objectives you've set in your roadmap. The key is to choose metrics that are not just measurable, but also directly related to the goals you want to achieve.

  

The SMART Criteria

When selecting KPIs, adhere to the SMART criteria to ensure they are:

- Specific: Clearly define what you are measuring.
    
- Measurable: Quantifiable and data-driven.
    
- Achievable: Realistic given your resources.
    
- Relevant: Directly related to your CoE objectives.
    
- Time-bound: Have a specific timeframe for achievement.
    

  

Example KPIs

Here are some example KPIs that could be relevant for a Center of Excellence focused on AI:

  

AI Adoption Rate: Measures the percentage of departments actively using AI technologies. 

-    Objective: Achieve 80% adoption across all departments within one year.
    

  

Model Accuracy: Monitors the accuracy of AI tools used by the CoE.

- Objective: Establish a baseline measure for the number of hallucinations a tool generates.
    

  

Employee Upskilling: Tracks the number of employees who have completed AI training programs.

- Objective: Train # of employees in AI basics within the next quarter.
    

  

Project Turnaround Time: Measures the average time taken to complete AI projects.

- Objective: Reduce project turnaround time by 20% in the next six months.
    

  

Stakeholder Satisfaction: Gauges stakeholder satisfaction through periodic surveys.

- Objective: Achieve growing stakeholder satisfaction rate of at least 10% in the next 6 months
    

  

Cost Savings: Monitors the reduction in operational costs due to AI implementation.

- Objective: Achieve a 15% reduction in operational costs within the next fiscal year.

#### Data Collection for KPIs

  

Data collection is the backbone of any KPI monitoring system. Without reliable and accurate data, your KPIs are just numbers without context. The type of data you collect should be directly aligned with your identified KPIs and should be actionable, meaning it should provide insights that you can act upon to improve performance.

  

Types of Data to Collect

1. Quantitative Data: This includes numerical data like project completion rates, model accuracy percentages, and cost savings. These are straightforward to measure and analyze.
    
2. Qualitative Data: This includes non-numerical data like employee and stakeholder feedback, which can be gathered through surveys or interviews.
    
3. Temporal Data: This involves data that is time-bound, such as project timelines, which can help in assessing efficiency over periods.
    
4. Comparative Data: This includes data that can be compared against industry benchmarks or past performance metrics within the organization.
    

  

Most Actionable Data

- User Engagement: Measures how frequently and effectively AI tools are being used within the organization.
    
- Error Rates: Monitors the number of errors or issues reported in AI projects, which can be a strong indicator of the quality of work.
    
- ROI Metrics: Return on investment metrics can provide a clear picture of the financial benefits of your AI initiatives.
    

  

Monitoring and Reporting

Establish a regular cadence for reviewing your KPIs. This could be monthly or quarterly reviews with your CoE team and stakeholders. Use these reviews to make data-driven decisions and course corrections.

### Case Study: NASA’s AI Center of Excellence

### Glossary of Terms

  

Center of Excellence (CoE) - A team or organizational unit that guides and governs AI strategies and initiatives across the organization.

  

AI Champions - Members of the CoE who are driving forces and subject matter experts in AI adoption.

  

Centralized Model - A CoE structure where all AI resources are grouped into a single, centralized team.

  

Decentralized Model - A CoE structure where AI resources are distributed across different business units and teams. 

  

Hub-and-Spoke Model - A hybrid CoE structure with a central hub guiding strategy and spokes within departments driving specialized initiatives.

  

Roadmap - A detailed plan outlining the CoE's objectives, milestones, timeframes, responsibilities and resources required. 

  

Gap Analysis - Comparing current state capabilities with future needs to identify resource gaps when implementing a CoE.

  

KPIs - Key Performance Indicators used to quantitatively measure the progress and success of the CoE initiatives.

  

SMART Criteria - Guidelines for KPIs to be Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound.

  

Qualitative Data - Non-numerical feedback data collected through surveys, interviews, focus groups used for CoE monitoring.

  

Quantitative Data - Numerical metrics collected by the CoE to gauge progress towards goals.
