### Step 1: Conduct Strategy Review

Analyzing your current business strategy and identifying specific opportunities to leverage AI is crucial for ensuring your AI efforts align to core goals and deliver value. 

  

Follow this step-by-step guide:

  

1. Gather Inputs
    

- Assemble your current strategic plans, roadmaps, and key performance indicators (KPIs).
    
- Include past analysis such as SWOT, PESTEL, etc.
    
- Identify key stakeholders to interview. Below are some suggestions.
    

- A Data expert who knows what data is available
    
- A Business expert who can ensure use cases alignment to business goals
    
- A Subject matter expert provides domain knowledge in the area(s) you want to incorporate AI
    
- A Technical expert who will implement the AI
    
- A Supervisory expert who will likely oversee implementation.
    

  

2. Questions to Consider:
    

- What are your 3-5 year strategic priorities and goals?
    
- What KPIs indicate the success of your strategy?
    
- What external factors may impact your strategic trajectory?
    

  

3. Map your Value Chain
    

- Visually map out your key processes end-to-end.
    
- Include inputs, outputs, and flow of data/information.
    
- Identify pain points and bottlenecks.
    
- Questions to Consider:
    

- What are our core business processes?
    
- Where do you (or your stakeholders) see the biggest pain points or inefficiencies?
    
- Where does communication/information flow break down?
    

  

4. Pinpoint AI Opportunities
    

- Analyze each process through the lens of how AI could impact it.
    
- Brainstorm ideas and quantify potential value where possible.
    
- Prioritize high-impact opportunities aligned to strategic goals.
    
- Questions to Consider:
    

- Where could AI drive automation to reduce costs?
    
- How could AI generate insights from our data?
    
- What innovative AI capabilities could differentiate you?
    
- Which opportunities have the highest ROI potential?
    
- What tools do you already have that can easily incorporate AI?
    

  

5. Define Pilots
    

- Based on priority AI opportunities, define potential pilot projects.
    
- Outline business case, resources required, success metrics, and risks.
    
- Questions to Consider:
    

- What is the minimum viable pilot we can implement to validate ROI?
    
- What resources do we need in terms of data, expertise, infrastructure?
    
- How will we measure success and value achieved?

Technical Expert

Supervisory Expert

- Broader view across business and technology

- Oversees initiative progress and milestones

### Step 4: Drafting Preliminary AI Values

Defining your organization's AI values and principles is not just an abstract exercise - it is becoming a business imperative. Laws governing AI ethics are rapidly evolving. And without establishing your North Star for AI adoption, you risk adverse impacts from technological missteps or public mistrust.

  

News stories of biased algorithms and other AI harms have raised societal concerns. Lawmakers are responding with an array of regulations on transparency, fairness, and safety. Adoption without principled guardrails opens your organization to legal jeopardy.

  

More so, AI advances can fuel a reckless sense of technological manifest destiny. But just because a capability exists does not mean it should be pursued without conscious oversight. Your organization must stay grounded in its ethics amidst AI's breakneck pace.

  

Establishing clear AI values aligns your initiatives to strategy while upholding moral boundaries. This distinguishes responsible stewardship from reckless disruption. Your principles become the compass to navigate AI's complex promise and risks.

  

With thoughtful guidance, your people can adopt AI mindfully. They evolve from bystanders to active participants steering this powerful technology for good. But this requires leadership in sculpting an AI future that aligns with your organizational values. The time to start is now.

  

However, the process for drafting values differs across organizations:

  

Democratic/Inclusive

Some cultures favor highly inclusive processes with extensive committees and organizational surveys to define values. This builds broad buy-in but moves slowly.

  

1. Assemble a diverse AI values committee: Include representatives across business units, job levels, demographics, etc.
    

2. Conduct organization-wide surveys: Gauge attitudes, concerns, and perspectives on AI and ethics.
    

3. Hold interactive workshops: Facilitate collaborative sessions to brainstorm draft values.
    

4. Open comment period: Share the draft values company-wide for feedback and refinements.
    

5. Finalize through committee vote: Committee reviews all feedback and finalizes values through majority vote.
    

6. Leadership endorsement: Get leadership alignment to officially endorse the final AI values.
    

7. Ongoing reviews: Re-survey periodically to determine if updates are needed.
    

  

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/ik1qFmXSsZO8ipRTQcPaMwtyi9UqUChZ1R94PfBhDBn50kXgZgsDOhnsAS1azVquknDxyyFTBUZuIUKEcmEGsoJQsEchfS265A43gDaAAqRdjXRozZNtYwIHtDyz1PX2BR-2_uxZtc2CnTer9g-f5vI)

  

This inclusive approach surfaces a diversity of internal viewpoints and builds broad ownership for the AI values across the organization. But it requires patience as reaching consensus can take time.

  

Top-Down Directive

Other cultures have top-down leadership that drafts and hands down AI principles unilaterally. This is faster but risks less ownership across the company.

  

1. Leadership analysis: CEO and senior leadership analyze business strategy, risks, and ethics.
    

2. Initial draft: CEO or delegated team drafts initial set of AI principles.
    

3. Expert advisory input: If possible, solicit input on draft principles from an advisory board, or consultants.
    
4. Legal/compliance review: Cross-check draft principles for regulatory and compliance alignment.
    

5. Leadership finalization: CEO finalizes principles based on reviews and inputs.
    

6. Internal announcement: Leadership formally announces and shares final AI principles company-wide.
    

7. Ongoing refinement: Leadership periodically refines principles as AI strategy evolves.
    

  

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/0V04aF5X7gUs6UlYUhkjfO64Zt2iANnGqW2age-sdSx-QIzxht45arUc7SfJp_RsLD_x4n3U-6DiA3P-MPUB3o-PKdXn65Ry0vdwcGgomcTBzjLhubQM3LSjLqP8-SoA6NEJ9sUqNAE4RErlpaGcdyk)

  

This top-down approach enables rapid drafting of AI values aligned to business objectives. But less inclusion means less co-creation with the organization. Ongoing engagement is key.

  

  

Hybrid Approach

A hybrid approach leverages leadership direction combined with some inclusion of stakeholder perspectives. It balances speed with input.

  

1. AI team drafts initial set: Cross-functional AI team creates initial draft based on strategy and ethics.
    

2. Leadership alignment: Leadership reviews and aligns on the initial set of values.
    

3. Stakeholder focus groups: Conduct focus group sessions with key stakeholders for input.
    

4. Employee survey: Distribute company-wide pulse survey for broad feedback.
    

5. AI team refinement: AI team synthesizes inputs into refined set of values.
    

6. Legal/compliance review: Cross-check refined values for any regulatory issues.
    

7. Leadership finalization: Leadership approves the final values for release.
    

8. Company-wide announcement: Share the final values and provide ongoing engagement opportunities.
    

  

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/AphjG3wWWIxmpnKkIXf1JY6_lx1OfTHSR0I88Aim6AEg7wQBREQCJItoiveFhr5RvVIdnbUugPpnGHvdelBZT7MSjyRwoikKftNprw8syIrqW0_-71EPE9ZrGi-GDsToobHNHxTE1mpDivHOtSaOTv4)

  

This hybrid approach balances speed by starting with top-down direction, along with inclusion by soliciting broad stakeholder perspectives. It allows thoughtful input while avoiding gridlock.

  

Core Considerations

Regardless of approach, ensure your values align to business strategy and ethical priorities while resonating with the organizational culture. Appointing the AI team to draft a starting point can accelerate the process.

  

While the drafting process varies - democratic, top-down or hybrid - taking the time to define values is always crucial. This establishes the DNA that will shape your overall AI journey.

### Step 5: Implement and Monitor

Starting small is key when it comes to launching pilot projects with generative AI, such as large language models or image generation. For instance, before fully committing to auto-generating content, you could trial the technology on a minor project like producing weekly blog posts or summaries. The idea is to select a project that won't interfere with your existing operations but is substantial enough to provide valuable insights.

  

Keeping Tabs

In your initial pilot projects, monitoring is key. Beyond keeping an eye on your performance metrics, you'll want to align these with the KPIs you've previously defined. Imagine you've launched a pilot using a large language model to auto-generate customer service responses. One of your KPIs might be customer satisfaction rates. You can then assess the pilot's success by comparing generated responses to customer feedback.

  

Moreover, don't simply measure what's going well; pay attention to what's falling short. If your generative AI isn't meeting your KPIs, it's crucial to be ready to put a hold on the pilot until adjustments can be made. This could involve refining the AI model, adjusting the parameters, or rethinking your goals altogether.

  

Measure Twice, Cut Once

Evaluation is the next crucial step, and it's vital to align the pilot project with both your AI values and your predefined KPIs. Suppose one of your KPIs is maintaining a high level of data accuracy. In that case, you should assess the generative AI's performance against this specific metric. It's beneficial to have a values-alignment checklist tailored to each pilot, which works in tandem with your KPIs for a comprehensive evaluation.

  

Make sure to actively involve your AI team in the ongoing assessment of these pilot projects. Regular consultations with the team can provide insights into how well the pilot aligns with both KPIs and your organizational values. Establish a schedule for these periodic reviews to ensure continuity and consistent alignment.

  

Iterate

After you've launched your pilot and monitored it against your KPIs, the learning doesn't stop there. The next phase is all about iterative improvement. For instance, if you started with a large language model to draft marketing emails, take the lessons you've learned and apply them to a broader range of content, like social media posts or customer FAQs. Refine your AI's algorithms, data sets, and other variables to make it even more aligned with your values and objectives.

  

Importantly, don't keep these lessons siloed within the team that executed the pilot. Share your findings across the organization, as lessons learned in one department can often be applied in another. This encourages a culture of continuous learning and improvement, which is vital for the successful implementation of AI.

### Step 2: Policy Framework

Creating a policy framework is a collaborative effort that requires inputs from various departments and stakeholders within your organization. This is not a task for a single individual or department; it's a collective endeavor.

  

Who Needs to be Involved

- Legal Team: To ensure compliance with laws and regulations.
    
- AI Team: If possible, you should work with the stakeholders already involved in your pilot, as they will be the most knowledgeable to align the policies with organizational values.
    
- IT and other Technical Experts: To provide insights into the capabilities and limitations of the AI technologies you are using, and model implementation through new or existing tools.
    
- HR Department: To understand the impact on employees and organizational culture.
    
- Customer Relations: To gauge the impact on clients or end-users.
    

  

Framework Template Components:

  

A Policy Framework typically includes the following sections:

1. Introduction: Briefly describe the purpose of the AI policies and how they align with organizational values.
    
2. Scope: Define the range of activities, departments, and technologies that the policies will cover.
    
3. Objectives: Clearly state what you aim to achieve with these policies.
    
4. Responsibilities: Assign roles and responsibilities for implementing and monitoring the policies.
    
5. Compliance Measures: Outline the steps for ensuring compliance, including audits and reporting mechanisms.
    
6. Enforcement Mechanisms: Describe the consequences of non-compliance, which could range from warnings to legal actions.
    
7. Review and Update Procedures: Specify how often the policies will be reviewed and the process for making updates.
    
8. Definitions: Provide definitions for any technical or specialized terms used in the policies.

### Step 3: Legal and Ethical Review

  

After you've drafted your AI policy framework, the next critical step is to have it reviewed by legal experts. This isn't just a formality; it's an essential part of ensuring that your policies are both ethical and legally compliant. 

  

Start by involving your in-house legal team if you have one. Their familiarity with your organization's operations will be invaluable. If you don't have an in-house team, or if you're dealing with specialized areas of AI and technology law, consider bringing in external legal consultants. They can provide the specialized expertise you might not have in-house.

  

The legal review will focus on several key areas. First, there's compliance. Your policies must align with all applicable laws and regulations, whether they're local, state, or federal. This is especially important if you operate in multiple jurisdictions or have a diverse customer base. 

  

Next, consider potential liabilities. What are the legal risks associated with your AI's actions? This could range from data breaches to unintended discriminatory practices. Your legal team will help you identify these risks and ensure they're clearly outlined in your policies.

  

Data protection is another crucial area. If your AI handles personal data, your policies must be in line with data protection laws like the GDPR in Europe or the CCPA in California. Make sure you're transparent about how data will be used and protected.

  

Don't forget about intellectual property. If your AI interacts with user-generated content, you'll need policies that address IP concerns. This is particularly important for organizations that use AI in creative or content-generating capacities.

  

Lastly, transparency and disclosure are key. Your policies should clearly state what the AI can and cannot do, and how data is used and stored. This is not just good practice; in many cases, it's legally required.

  

And remember, the legal landscape is ever-changing, especially in the field of AI. So, this won't be a one-time review. Regular updates and legal check-ins are a must.

### Step 4: Communicating Change

  

Once your AI policies have been legally vetted and approved, it's time to share them with the people who will be most affected: your employees and your customers. This is not just about dissemination; it's about engagement and education.

  

Communicating to Employees

### Case Study: IBM and Responsible AI

Since Generative AI is only a year old as of writing this course, there are few reliable case studies related to its implementation, but many companies have already developed relevant and worthwhile frameworks and systems in order to manage AI at their companies. IBM in particular has done a great job of taking a thoughtful and organizational approach to embedding responsible AI into their DNA.

  

IBM has a rich history of focusing on ethics and values in technology development. This foundation was reinforced in 2003 when the company went through an introspective process to redefine its core values, landing on three pillars: dedication to client success, innovation that benefits society, and building trust in all relationships.

  

In 2015, IBM doubled down on its commitment to ethical technology by creating a leadership role focused solely on AI ethics. The new leader, Francesca Rossi, convened a group of employees to make concrete commitments around advancing the field of ethical AI. This led to the formation of an internal AI Ethics Board to guide governance, implementing ethics education across the company, establishing a research program on AI ethics and society, and engaging externally with stakeholders on responsible AI.

  

![](https://lh7-us.googleusercontent.com/1caAofnJXrGlBcg9oh31pgUkLfnIsmMyWGIJ2fAzZPPRY89RiJh9Qvo0FNRTuA1Qte8JuDqD5JhGW7FuP2PpU5WpR6P8YH_NieB9rZNAYbAbzQ8zex7U4iYyX0yaiEjwz5wwyQ-D2evAL8pjcFfYca4)

  

Three years later in 2018, IBM published its Principles for Trust and Transparency, staking out an ethical vision for AI centered on augmenting rather than replacing human intelligence, ensuring data ownership stays with creators, and designing AI systems that are transparent and explainable. To implement these principles, the company established a formal AI Ethics Board with representation from leaders across business units to provide centralized governance.

  

Meanwhile, IBM Research has been prolific in publishing over 200 papers on AI fairness, explainability, and other trustworthy AI topics. They translated research insights into open source toolkits aligned with IBM's pillars of trust in AI - fairness, explainability, robustness, and transparency.

  

Internally, IBM moved quickly to mandate ethics training and provide educational resources for all employees working with AI. The company also promoted diversity and inclusion, including through partnerships on AI ethics with external groups in academia, industry, government, and civil society. This multi-stakeholder approach reflects IBM's belief that a broad perspective is required to steer AI toward benefitting society

  

[Read the Full Case Study Here](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Responsible_Use_of_Technology_The_IBM_Case_Study_2021.pdf)

  

Although few companies have the resources available to fully match IBM, below are some potential recommendations based on this case study:

  

- Appoint an executive level leader responsible for overseeing AI, with an eye toward responsible use.
    
- Develop a set of ethical principles tailored to our company and products. Engage diverse voices in drafting.
    
- Form an ethics governance board with cross-functional representation to advise on issues and oversee efforts.
    
- Make ethics training mandatory for all employees working with AI, tailored to their roles.
    
- Perform AI ethics impact assessments on existing high-risk products and integrate assessments into new product development processes.
    
- Research and adopt tools that help assess algorithmic fairness, explainability, etc. Consider open sourcing some of these to promote industry transparency.
    
- Seek diverse perspectives through partnerships with civil society, academic researchers, and affected communities.
    
- Report publicly on your responsible AI efforts, being transparent about challenges and opportunities.

### Glossary of Terms

AI Values: Ethical principles and priorities guiding an organization's AI endeavors, serving as its moral compass during AI integration and operation.

  

AI Policies: Concrete rules, guidelines, and protocols established by an organization to operationalize and uphold its AI values.

  

AI Committee: A multidisciplinary team tasked with orchestrating an organization's AI strategy, encompassing the formulation of values and overseeing their realization.

  

Policy Framework: The foundational architecture of an organization's AI policies, detailing their ambit, goals, metrics, and execution methods.

  

Risk Assessment: A systematic evaluation of potential risks tied to various AI applications, considering elements like data vulnerability, user repercussions, and misuse likelihood.

  

Stakeholders: Entities and individuals, both internal and external to an organization, who possess a vested interest in or are influenced by AI integration. This encompasses technical units, legal counsel, and clientele.

  

Organizational Structure: The structural design governing an organization's AI operations, such as a centralized or decentralized model, influencing collaboration and operational efficiency.

  

Change Management: The orchestrated communication of alterations in policy or operational methods to stakeholders, pivotal for securing comprehension and endorsement.

  

Pilot Testing: The preliminary, small-scale deployment of AI policies or instruments to discern potential shortcomings and fine-tune strategies ahead of comprehensive implementation.

  

AI Roadmap: A forward-looking strategy delineating an organization's AI vision, key milestones, and implementation timelines, ensuring alignment with its overarching policies and values.

  

Monitoring and Evaluation: The consistent gathering of data and insights to gauge the efficacy of an organization's AI policies and tools, facilitating continuous refinement.
