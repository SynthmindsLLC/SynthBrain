Matching markets are a type of market where buyers and sellers are paired based on their preferences and a set of rules, rather than through price mechanisms alone. These markets are characterized by the need for mutual consent for a transaction to occur, making them distinct from commodity markets where goods are interchangeable and transactions are primarily price-driven.

Key concepts in matching markets include:

1. **Stability**: A matching is stable if there are no two agents who would both prefer to be matched with each other over their current matches. Stability is crucial for ensuring that once matches are made, they are not undermined by alternative, preferable arrangements.

2. **Optimality**: Depending on the context, optimality can refer to maximizing overall welfare, fairness, or other criteria defined by the market's design.

3. **Strategy-proofness**: A matching mechanism is strategy-proof if it is in every participant's best interest to report their true preferences. This property is essential for the mechanism to produce reliable outcomes.

Matching markets are found in various real-world applications, such as:

- **Labor markets**, where employers and job seekers are matched.
- **School admissions**, where students are matched with schools based on preferences.
- **Organ donation**, where donors and recipients are matched.
- **Roommate matching** and **marriage markets**, where individuals are matched based on mutual preferences.

The Gale-Shapley algorithm, also known as the Deferred Acceptance algorithm, is a famous solution to the stable marriage problem, demonstrating how to achieve stable matches efficiently. This algorithm and its variants have been applied in many areas, including the design of the National Resident Matching Program for matching medical residents to hospitals in the United States.

Matching markets highlight the importance of market design and the role of algorithms in facilitating efficient and fair outcomes in situations where price alone cannot determine allocations.
