# László Babai

László Babai is a Hungarian professor of computer science and mathematics at the University of Chicago. His research has significantly impacted the fields of theoretical computer science and discrete mathematics, particularly in computational complexity theory, algorithms, combinatorics, and finite groups. Babai is known for his work on the interactions between these fields and for introducing concepts such as Las Vegas algorithms and interactive proofs.

## Graph Isomorphism and Quasi-Polynomial Time Algorithm

Babai has made notable contributions to the graph isomorphism problem, which is the challenge of determining whether two finite graphs are isomorphic. In November 2015, he announced a breakthrough by developing a "quasi-polynomial time" algorithm for the graph isomorphism problem. This algorithm represented a significant improvement over the previous best algorithm, which had been unchallenged for more than 30 years. Babai's algorithm had a running time of $$2^{O((\log n)^c)}$$ for some fixed $$c>0$$, where $$n$$ is the number of vertices in the graph[1][2][6].

However, in January 2017, Babai retracted the quasi-polynomial claim after an error was discovered by mathematician Harald Helfgott. Babai then announced a correction and restored the quasi-polynomial claim, with Helfgott confirming the fix[2][6]. This development was considered a significant advancement in the field of computational complexity and graph theory.

## Contributions and Recognition

Babai's work extends beyond the graph isomorphism problem. He has contributed to various areas of mathematics and computer science, including group theory, where he was a pioneer in algorithmic group theory, developing techniques for numerous fundamental algorithmic problems on groups[9][17]. His contributions have been recognized with several awards, including the prestigious Knuth Prize in 2015 and the Gödel Prize in 1993[12]. Babai has also been elected a fellow of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences[12][17].

Babai's influence as an educator is also notable, having advised many Ph.D. students and contributed to the academic community through his teaching and mentorship[1][9].
