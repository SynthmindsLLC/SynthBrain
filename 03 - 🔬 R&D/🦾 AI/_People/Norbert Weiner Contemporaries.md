Norbert Wiener, a pivotal figure in the development of cybernetics and a precursor to modern artificial intelligence (AI), collaborated with and influenced several contemporaries at MIT and beyond in the fields of computer science, mathematics, and cognitive science. Among his notable contemporaries and collaborators related to AI were:

- **[[Warren Sturgis McCulloch]]**: A neurophysiologist whose work with Walter Pitts on the logical calculus of neural networks laid foundational concepts for neural computing and AI. Wiener's interactions with McCulloch were significant in the early days of exploring the mathematical and theoretical underpinnings of what would become AI[1].

- **[[Walter Pitts]]**: A logician and co-author with McCulloch of the seminal paper "A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity." This paper proposed the first mathematical model of a neural network, influencing the development of AI and cognitive science. Pitts was part of the research team at MIT recruited by Wiener[1].

- **[[Julian Bigelow]]**: An engineer who collaborated with Wiener on the development of anti-aircraft technology during World War II. Their work on predicting the flight paths of enemy aircraft using feedback systems contributed to Wiener's formulation of cybernetics, a concept central to AI's development[1].

- **[[Arturo Rosenblueth]]**: A physiologist and collaborator with Wiener and Bigelow on the 1943 article "Behavior, Purpose and Teleology," which explored the application of feedback mechanisms to both biological and mechanical systems. This work is considered a precursor to the field of cybernetics and, by extension, AI[1].

- **[[John von Neumann]]**: Although not directly mentioned as a contemporary at MIT, von Neumann's work on the architecture of computers and his contributions to game theory and systems theory were influential to Wiener and the broader development of AI. Wiener's cybernetic ideas and von Neumann's architectural principles for computing machines laid the groundwork for future AI research[1][2].

- **[[Claude Shannon]]**: Known as the "father of information theory," Shannon's work at MIT overlapped with Wiener's interests in communication and control. While Shannon was more directly associated with Bell Labs, his interactions with Wiener and the MIT community were influential in the early theoretical development of AI[5].

These individuals, among others, were integral to the multidisciplinary approach that characterized the early exploration of AI, combining insights from mathematics, engineering, biology, and philosophy. Wiener's work and collaborations at MIT played a crucial role in setting the stage for the development of AI as a field of study.
