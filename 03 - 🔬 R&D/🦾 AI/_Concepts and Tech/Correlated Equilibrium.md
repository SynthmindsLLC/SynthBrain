Correlated equilibrium is a solution concept in game theory that generalizes the concept of Nash equilibrium. Introduced by Robert Aumann in 1974, it allows for the possibility that players' choices can be correlated with each other, rather than being made independently[1].

In a correlated equilibrium, a third party, often referred to as a mediator, suggests a strategy to each player based on a probability distribution over the set of possible strategies. Players commit to following the mediator's suggestions, and the suggested strategies form a correlated equilibrium if no player has an incentive to unilaterally deviate from the suggested strategy, given the strategies of the others[1][2][3].

For example, in the game of Chicken, where two players can either "Dare" or "Chicken out," the correlated equilibrium can be achieved by a mediator randomly suggesting strategies to the players with certain probabilities. If the mediator suggests "Chicken out" to both players with a higher probability, it can lead to a higher expected payoff for both players compared to the mixed strategy Nash equilibrium[1][2].

Correlated equilibria can be computed using linear programming, and they exist in all finite games. They are also a superset of Nash equilibria, meaning every Nash equilibrium is also a correlated equilibrium, but not every correlated equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium[2][4][7].

The concept of correlated equilibrium is useful because it can lead to outcomes with higher payoffs than those achievable under Nash equilibrium and can be used to model situations where players are able to coordinate their strategies through some form of communication or signals[1][2][3][4].
