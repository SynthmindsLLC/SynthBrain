- Summary: OpenAI transcribed over a million hours of YouTube videos to train GPT-4 due to a lack of training data. The AI industry faces challenges in obtaining sufficient data for model training, leading to potential legal and ethical concerns.

## Highlights
- Earlier this week, *The Wall Street* journal reported that AI companies were running into a wall when it comes to gathering high-quality training data. Today, [*The New York Times* detailed](https://www.nytimes.com/2024/04/06/technology/tech-giants-harvest-data-artificial-intelligence.html?smid=nytcore-ios-share&sgrp=c-cb) some of the ways companies have dealt with this. Unsurprisingly, it involves doing things that fall into the hazy gray area of [AI copyright law](https://www.theverge.com/23444685/generative-ai-copyright-infringement-legal-fair-use-training-data). ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htvy7qhagvvavprjsq2q3w2v))
- OpenAI which, desperate for training data, reportedly developed its [Whisper audio transcription model](https://www.theverge.com/2022/9/23/23367296/openai-whisper-transcription-speech-recognition-open-source) to get over the hump, transcribing over a million hours of YouTube videos to train GPT-4, its most advanced large language model. That’s [according to *The New York Times*](https://www.nytimes.com/2024/04/06/technology/tech-giants-harvest-data-artificial-intelligence.html?smid=nytcore-ios-share&sgrp=c-cb), which reports that the company knew this was legally questionable but believed it to be fair use. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htvy8ayx2wwps5xad97as796))
- OpenAI president Greg Brockman was personally involved in collecting videos that were used, the *Times* writes. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htvyb54fy3z8yk3n0ajhp67m))
- company uses “numerous sources including publicly available data and partnerships for non-public data,” and that it’s looking into generating its own synthetic data. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htvybjnsgypcwa0vpfttq33y))
- The *Times* article says that the company exhausted supplies of useful data in 2021, and discussed transcribing YouTube videos, podcasts, and audiobooks after blowing through other resources. By then, it had trained its models on data that included computer code from Github, chess move databases, and schoolwork content from Quizlet. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htvyc47p2j1egtxpz3xxtebw))
- Google spokesperson Matt Bryant told *The Verge* in an email the company has “seen unconfirmed reports” of OpenAI’s activity, adding that “both our robots.txt files and Terms of Service prohibit unauthorized scraping or downloading of YouTube content,” echoing the [company’s terms of use](https://go.skimresources.com/?id=1025X1701640&xs=1&url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.youtube.com%2Fstatic%3Ftemplate%3Dterms&xcust=___vg__p_23886956__t_w__d_D). YouTube CEO Neal Mohan [said similar things](https://www.theverge.com/2024/4/4/24121463/openai-training-sora-on-youtube-videos-would-violate-the-platforms-rules) about the possibility that OpenAI used YouTube to train its Sora video-generating model this week. Bryant said Google takes “technical and legal measures” to prevent such unauthorized use “when we have a clear legal or technical basis to do so.” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htvyczhz6tjqkevwgbyda5tz))
- The *Times* writes that Google’s legal department asked the company’s privacy team to tweak its policy language to expand what it could do with consumer data, such as its office tools like Google Docs. The new policy was reportedly intentionally released on July 1st to take advantage of the distraction of the Independence Day holiday weekend. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htvydv320nryp473wva5h6px))
- Meta likewise bumped against the limits of good training data availability, and in recordings the *Times* heard, its AI team discussed its unpermitted use of copyrighted works while working to catch up to OpenAI. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htvye94cwx6frhdcd5b0qf3n))
- The company, after going through “almost available English-language book, essay, poem and news article on the internet,” apparently considered taking steps like paying for book licenses or even buying a large publisher outright. It was also apparently limited in the ways it could use consumer data by privacy-focused changes it made in the wake of the [Cambridge Analytica scandal](https://www.theverge.com/2018/4/10/17165130/facebook-cambridge-analytica-scandal). ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htvyewjq37e18kg2634aw6hw))

But the companies’ other option is using whatever they can find, whether they have permission or not, and based on [multiple](https://www.theverge.com/2023/10/19/23924246/mike-huckabee-books-lawsuit-generative-ai-microsoft-meta-bloomberg-eleutherai-books3) [lawsuits](https://www.theverge.com/2023/9/20/23882140/george-r-r-martin-lawsuit-openai-copyright-infringement) [filed](https://www.theverge.com/2023/9/11/23869145/writers-sue-openai-chatgpt-copyright-claims) [in](https://www.theverge.com/2024/2/28/24085973/intercept-raw-story-alternet-openai-lawsuit-copyright) [the](https://www.theverge.com/2024/2/13/24072131/sarah-silverman-paul-tremblay-openai-chatgpt-copyright-lawsuit) [last](https://www.theverge.com/2024/3/5/24091719/microsoft-new-york-times-openai-motion-dismiss-copyright-lawsuit) [year](https://www.theverge.com/2023/2/6/23587393/ai-art-copyright-lawsuit-getty-images-stable-diffusion) or so, that way is, let’s say, more than a little fraught.
