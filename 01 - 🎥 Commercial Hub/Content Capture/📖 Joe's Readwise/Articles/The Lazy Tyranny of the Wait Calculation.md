## Highlights
- waiting for technology to get better beats acting immediately. This is called a [Wait Calculation](https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/2006-kennedy.pdf), and it was first used for the Barnard’s Star scenario, but the logic also turns out to apply to other problems as well. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hm9kknzx4sknhazvjpwht04x))
- AI has all the characteristics of a technology that requires a Wait Calculation. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hm9km2s2rp7mwzwb20cbjnmf))
- Over nearly a decade, I have been helping build Wharton Interactive, where I have been working with a team of talented people spending building games and simulations to teach key business skills. Each game took a tremendous amount of time and work, so we spent a huge amount of effort developing toolkits to make it easier for people to create pedagogically-grounded teaching games.
  We could have waited. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hm9kr45323zsqn2b4mbgq7x5))
- Another way to look at the *how good* and *how fast* questions is to look at prediction markets, where people bet on the dates that particular events will occur. These are often surprisingly accurate, and one of the key markets puts the date of [“weak” AGI](https://www.metaculus.com/questions/3479/date-weakly-general-ai-is-publicly-known/) (at the 75th percentile of human abilities across subjects) as happening in a little over two years, and [full AGI](https://www.metaculus.com/questions/5121/date-of-artificial-general-intelligence/) within a decade. Again, you don’t have to believe these predictions, but it is worth including them in your Wait Calculation. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hm9kv5gxcgkdxz62a5vn77vc))
