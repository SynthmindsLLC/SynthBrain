- Summary: TechCrunch is highlighting women who have made significant contributions to the AI field with a series of interviews. The piece discusses the gender gap in AI and points out notable women who have been pioneers in the industry, despite the lack of recognition they often receive. Studies show a wide gender gap in the AI workforce, with challenges such as discrimination and unequal opportunities for women. The article emphasizes the importance of strong mentorship, commitment, and policy changes to promote diversity and support women in AI, aiming to address the disparities that exist within the field.

## Highlights
- Elaine Rich, a retired computer scientist formerly at the University of Texas at Austin, published one of the first textbooks on AI in 1983, and later went on to become the director of a corporate AI lab in 1988. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hq1sy8641gzaz6vehv09gqgb))
- Harvard professor Cynthia Dwork made waves decades ago in the fields of AI fairness, [differential privacy](https://techcrunch.com/2022/02/24/implement-differential-privacy-to-power-up-data-sharing-and-cooperation/) and distributed computing. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hq1syevg5nh6qbn10sgqmc5p))
- And Cynthia Breazeal, a roboticist and professor at MIT and the co-founder of [Jibo](https://techcrunch.com/2019/03/04/the-lonely-death-of-jibo-the-social-robot/), the robotics startup, worked to develop one of the earliest “social robots,” Kismet, in the late ’90s and early 2000s. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hq1syr00xxe8rytfbkj86f3k))
- According to a 2021 Stanford [study](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/ai-index-diversity-report-unmoving-needle), just 16% of tenure-track faculty focused on AI are women. [In a separate study](https://www.weforum.org/agenda/2021/08/5-ways-increase-women-working-ai/) released the same year by the World Economic Forum, the co-authors find that women only hold 26% of analytics-related and AI positions. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hq1sz5b4j8mn8yqd2dscp5sa))
- In worse news, the gender gap in AI is widening — not narrowing. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hq1szdrkhf0ybm8xzv3y5a3y))
- Nesta, the U.K.’s innovation agency for social good, conducted [a 2019 analysis](https://www.nesta.org.uk/press-release/gender-diversity-crisis-ai-less-14-ai-researchers-are-women-numbers-decreasing-over-last-10-years/) that concluded that the proportion of AI academic papers co-authored by at least one woman hadn’t improved since the 1990s. As of 2019, just 13.8% of the AI research papers on Arxiv.org, a repository for preprint scientific papers, were authored or co-authored by women, with the numbers steadily decreasing over the preceding decade. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hq1szv8n19c5dd098998a0p8))
- But [a Deloitte survey of women in AI](https://www2.deloitte.com/content/dam/Deloitte/cn/Documents/deloitte-analytics/cn-consulting-women-in-ai-en.pdf) highlights a few of the more prominent (and obvious) ones, including judgment from male peers and discrimination as a result of not fitting into established male-dominated molds in AI. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hq1t054d5yxh13vcfyxzd1pw))
- It starts in college: 78% of women responding to the Deloitte survey said they didn’t have a chance to intern in AI or machine learning while they were undergraduates. Over half (58%) said they ended up leaving at least one employer because of how men and women were treated differently, while 73% considered leaving the tech industry altogether due to unequal pay and an inability to advance in their careers. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hq1t0nenp7tjtg3jaqn2srpk))
