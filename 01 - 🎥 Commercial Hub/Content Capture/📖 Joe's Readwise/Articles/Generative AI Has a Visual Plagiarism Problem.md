## Highlights
- Recent empirical work has shown that LLMs are in some instances capable of reproducing, or reproducing with minor changes, substantial chunks of text that appear in their training sets. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbk1yxzf8nn6c30986q55c))
- a [2023 paper](https://not-just-memorization.github.io/extracting-training-data-from-chatgpt.html) by Milad Nasr and colleagues showed that LLMs can be prompted into dumping private information such as email address and phone numbers. Carlini and coauthors [recently showed](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.07646.pdf) that larger chatbot models (though not smaller ones) sometimes regurgitated large chunks of text verbatim. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbkf49bm68w9xcqvt64gsf))
- the [recent lawsuit](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/12/27/business/media/new-york-times-open-ai-microsoft-lawsuit.html) that *The New York Times* filed against OpenAI showed many examples in which OpenAI software recreated *New York Times* stories nearly verbatim ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbkt9xjwk1g9t3vzvdw4bn))

- We will call such near-verbatim outputs “plagiaristic outputs,” because *prima facie* if human created them we would call them instances of plagiarism. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbm8595srn4a0mv4dmenf3))
- The prevalence of plagiaristic responses likely depends heavily on factors such as the size of the model and the exact nature of the training set. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbneanz01fbb5m989r5pt9))
- quite a lot is riding on the very existence of these kinds of outputs—as well as on the outcome of that particular lawsuit, which could have significant financial and structural implications for the field of generative AI going forward. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbpwxbs468j66m4cdm8g91))
- Can image-generating models be induced to produce plagiaristic outputs based on copyright materials? ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbqb5pwdfk5hb29yb9ms92))
- Just before *The New York Times v. OpenAI* lawsuit was made public, we found that the answer is clearly yes, even without directly soliciting plagiaristic outputs. Here are some examples elicited from the “alpha” version of [Midjourney V6](https://www.midjourney.com/home?callbackUrl=%2Fexplore) by the [second author](https://rahll.carbonmade.com/) of this article, a visual artist who was worked on a number of major films (including *The Matrix Resurrections*, *Blue Beetle*, and *The Hunger Games*) with many of Hollywood’s best-known studios (including Marvel and Warner Bros.). ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbr0nzk8c23mrbq9rw4y4c))
- Southen found that it was in fact easy to generate many plagiaristic outputs, with brief prompts related to commercial films (prompts are shown). ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbr912rc0qm5skxkabcggv))
- it seems all but certain that Midjourney V6 has been trained on copyrighted materials ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbrnjjwd2nba1mrjf37x94))
- we also found [important related work](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.13188.pdf) by Carlini on visual images on the [Stable Diffusion](https://clipdrop.co/stable-diffusion?utm_campaign=stable_diffusion_promo&utm_medium=cta_button&utm_source=stability_ai) platform that converged on similar conclusions, albeit using a more complex, automated adversarial technique. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbs80gae1xxq79r21z0s4p))
- the most compelling part of *The New York Times* complaint is that the plaintiffs established that plagiaristic responses could be elicited without invoking *The New York Times* at all. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbt6ensk54dwjxhzt1bn65))
- Such examples are particularly compelling because they raise the possibility that an end user might *inadvertently* produce infringing materials. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbtqbg418hrrf5vfs95xw9))
- In our third experiment with Midjourney, we asked whether it was capable of evoking entire film frames, without direct instruction. Again, we found that the answer was yes. (The top one is from a Hot Toys shoot rather than a film.) ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbvmqzv1srah16c1k1t0jh))
- a prompt of just a single word (not counting routine parameters) that’s not specific to any film, character, or actor yielded apparently infringing content: that word was “screencap.” The images below were created with that prompt. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbw0cfe1w6e1w9dpdezh3j))
- a lawsuit has introduced a [spreadsheet](https://www.theregister.com/2024/01/04/midjourney_artists_spreadsheet/) attributed to Midjourney containing a list of more than 4,700 artists whose work is thought to have been used in training, quite possibly without consent. For further discussion of generative AI data scraping, see [Create Don’t Scrape](https://www.createdontscrape.com/). ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbwzhwjem3ya51t44jq3jm))
- Midjourney’s CEO was [interviewed by *Forbes*](https://www.forbes.com/sites/robsalkowitz/2022/09/16/midjourney-founder-david-holz-on-the-impact-of-ai-on-art-imagination-and-the-creative-economy/?sh=4c1fc9822d2b), expressing a certain lack of concern for the rights of copyright holders, saying in response to an interviewer who asked: “Did you seek consent from living artists or work still under copyright?”
  > No. There isn’t really a way to get a hundred million images and know where they’re coming from. It would be cool if images had metadata embedded in them about the copyright owner or something. But that’s not a thing; there’s not a registry. There’s no way to find a picture on the Internet, and then automatically trace it to an owner and then have any way of doing anything to authenticate it. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgby6ncteeg9tgnr1xwfd1a))
- Midjourney apparently sought to suppress our findings, banning one of this story’s authors after he reported his first results. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbz7whhvz4sve3vfsd0v07))
- In the United States, for example, a four-part doctrine of [fair use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/fair_use) allows potentially infringing works to be used in some instances, such as if the usage is brief and for the purposes of criticism, commentary, scientific evaluation, or parody. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgbzyb5f1rkpqy82ra0rqzj))
- Midjourney is a service that sells subscriptions, at large scale. An individual user might make a case with a particular instance of potential infringement that their specific use of, for example, a character from *Dune* was for satire or criticism, or their own noncommercial purposes. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgc0c74bfmwpswg1vmmbb1d))
- One user on X [pointed to the fact](https://twitter.com/gordic_aleksa/status/1742136689022103989) that Japan has allowed AI companies to train on copyright materials. While this observation is true, it is [incomplete and oversimplified](https://twitter.com/neilturkewitz/status/1742195405767459057), as that training is constrained by limitations on unauthorized use drawn directly from relevant international law (including the [Berne Convention](https://www.wipo.int/treaties/en/ip/berne/) and [TRIPS agreement](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/trips_e/intel2_e.htm)) ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgc11dtarxf7rk2ajw3nm22))
- Significantly, in the end, Napster’s infringement on a mass scale was shut down by the courts, after lawsuits by [Metallica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallica_v._Napster,_Inc.) and the [Recording Industry Association of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%26M_Records,_Inc._v._Napster,_Inc.) (RIAA). The new business model of streaming was launched, in which publishers and artists (to a much smaller degree than we would like) received a cut. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgc2bgg3hk2cb4tyze5zs93))
- Napster as people knew it essentially disappeared overnight; the company itself went bankrupt, with its assets, including its name, sold to a streaming service. We do not think that large generative AI companies should assume that the laws of copyright and trademark will inevitability be rewritten around their needs. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgc2rbbc2a48v6btdkkh3cv))
- Compounding these matters, we have discovered evidence that a senior software engineer at Midjourney took part in a conversation in February 2022 about how to evade copyright law by “[laundering” data](https://x.com/nelkmarge/status/1740223308304654514?s=61&t=2voLMkhJf6P349CqztWSAQ) “through a [fine tuned codex](https://microsoft.github.io/prompt-engineering/).” Another participant who may or may not have worked for Midjourney then said “at some point it really becomes impossible to trace what’s a derivative work in the eyes of copyright.” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgc3f9zeqgajvcekw55qvgc))
- sources have recently reported that Midjourney may have deliberately created an immense list of artists on which to train, perhaps without licensing or compensation. Given how close the current software seems to come to source materials, it’s not hard to envision a class action lawsuit. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgc3xmp8nqsghtezf40tetx))
- Midjourney apparently sought to suppress our findings, banning Southen (without even a refund) after he reported his first results, and again after he created a new account from which additional results were reported. It then apparently changed its [terms of service](https://docs.midjourney.com/docs/terms-of-service) just before Christmas by inserting new language: “You may not use the Service to try to violate the intellectual property rights of others, including copyright, patent, or trademark rights. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgc4ghzyf090nrf34p9raaf))
- We find these practices—banning users and discouraging red-teaming—unacceptable. The only way to ensure that tools are valuable, safe, and not exploitative is to allow the community an opportunity to investigate; this is precisely why the community has generally agreed that red-teaming is an important part of AI development, particularly because these systems are as yet far from fully understood. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgc5g2jhh0m7wqqmn7b1mva))
- Our next set of experiments asked whether what we found with respect to Midjourney was true on OpenAI’s [DALL-E 3](https://openai.com/dall-e-3), as made available through Microsoft’s Bing. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgc7y07b5gkc7mgpk8jxx0h))
- As we reported recently [on Substack](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/things-are-about-to-get-a-lot-worse), the answer was again clearly yes. As with Midjourney, DALL-E 3 was capable of creating plagiaristic (near identical) representations of trademarked characters, even when those characters were not mentioned by name. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgc86k6q6a23avjae8vza83))
- OpenAI seems to be well aware of the fact that their software might infringe on copyright, offering in November to [indemnify users](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2023/nov/06/openai-chatgpt-customers-copyright-lawsuits) (with some restrictions) from copyright infringement lawsuits. Given the scale of what we have uncovered here, the potential costs are considerable. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgc92b2wgzfsp4k9jntj73r))
- Specific copyrighted materials cannot in any simple way be removed from existing models; large neural networks are not databases in which an offending record can easily be deleted. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgca9yhsysyb5m2esh8s112))
- Already, there are online guides with advice on [how to outwit OpenAI’s guardrails](https://x.com/ai_for_success/status/1725457976885186956?s=61&t=2voLMkhJf6P349CqztWSAQ) for DALL-E 3, with advice like “Include specific details that distinguish the character, such as different hairstyles, facial features, and body textures” and “Employ color schemes that hint at the original but use unique shades, patterns, and arrangements.” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgccnehmvbdhpckrsmgv8h4))
- The very existence of potentially infringing outputs is evidence of another problem: the nonconsensual use of copyrighted human work to train machines. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgcea88bxxdzk8f8xjw73q3))
- Users should be able to expect that the software products they use will not cause them to infringe copyright. And in multiple examples currently [circulating], the user could not be expected to know that the model’s output was a copy of someone’s copyrighted work ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgcgyvhnstf4y0g35b8dqsj))
- In the words of risk analyst [Vicki Bier](https://twitter.com/biervicki/status/1741150952659300614?s=12),
  > “If the tool doesn’t warn the user that the output might be copyrighted how can the user be responsible? AI can help me infringe copyrighted material that I have never seen and have no reason to know is copyrighted.” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgch4wzwfbr83wrsf4t1ppm))
- Software engineer Frank Rundatz recently stated a [broader perspective](https://x.com/frankrundatz/status/1683462922389815297?s=61&t=2voLMkhJf6P349CqztWSAQ).
  > One day we’re going to look back and wonder how a company had the audacity to copy all the world’s information and enable people to violate the copyrights of those works. 
  > All Napster did was enable people to transfer files in a peer-to-peer manner. They didn’t even host any of the content! Napster even developed a system to stop 99.4% of copyright infringement from their users but were still shut down because the court required them to stop 100%. 
  > OpenAI scanned and hosts all the content, sells access to it and will even generate derivative works for their paying users. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgcjrgmjtsytpq807zrpn3q))
- Stanford Professor Surya Ganguli [adds](https://x.com/suryaganguli/status/1741322962249367776?s=61&t=2voLMkhJf6P349CqztWSAQ):
  > Many researchers I know in big tech are working on AI alignment to human values. But at a gut level, shouldn’t such alignment entail compensating humans for providing training data thru their original creative, copyrighted output? (This is a values question, not a legal one). ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hkgcjz0zg70ya9bk99qzfr4p))

To the extent that the AI community is serious about aligning software to human values, it’s imperative that laws, norms, and software be developed to combat such uses.
