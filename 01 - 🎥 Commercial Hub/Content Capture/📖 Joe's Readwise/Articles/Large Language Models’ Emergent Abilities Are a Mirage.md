# Large Language Models’ Emergent Abilities Are a Mirage

![rw-book-cover](https://media.wired.com/photos/65fcb020abfbcf31f180cf6d/master/pass/AbilitiesJumpMirage-byKristinaArmitage-Lede-scaled.jpg)

## Metadata
- Author: [[Stephen Ornes]]
- Date: 2024-03-24
- Full Title: Large Language Models’ Emergent Abilities Are a Mirage
- Category: #articles
- Summary: Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown remarkable abilities that some researchers consider emergent but a new study by Stanford University suggests that these abilities may not be as unpredictable as previously thought. The study challenges the idea of sudden breakthroughs in LLM capabilities, attributing them to how researchers measure performance rather than inherent emergent qualities. By analyzing tasks like arithmetic, the researchers demonstrate that abilities like addition may not emerge suddenly but rather gradually and predictably as the models scale up in complexity. While some debate remains among scientists regarding the nature of emergent abilities in LLMs, the study sheds light on the importance of refining metrics to understand and predict the behavior of these advanced language models.

## Highlights
- Two years ago, in a project called the [Beyond the Imitation Game benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04615), or BIG-bench, 450 researchers compiled a list of 204 tasks designed to test the capabilities of [large language models](https://www.wired.com/story/how-chatgpt-works-large-language-model/), which power chatbots like ChatGPT. On most tasks, performance improved predictably and smoothly as the models scaled up—the larger the model, the better it got. But with other tasks, the jump in ability wasn’t smooth. The performance remained near zero for a while, then performance jumped. Other studies found similar leaps in ability. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hss5n2mthc13n74aq9by4kd9))
- In [a paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07682) published in August 2022, researchers noted that these behaviors are not only surprising but unpredictable, and that they should inform the evolving conversations around AI [safety](https://www.wired.com/story/meet-the-humans-trying-to-keep-us-safe-from-ai/), potential, and risk. They called the abilities “[emergent](https://www.quantamagazine.org/the-unpredictable-abilities-emerging-from-large-ai-models-20230316/),” a word that describes collective behaviors that only appear once a system reaches a high level of complexity. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hss6hj6jx5hm503bmhqr7fkx))
- [A new paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.15004) by a trio of researchers at Stanford University posits that the sudden appearance of these abilities is just a consequence of the way researchers measure the LLM’s performance. The abilities, they argue, are neither unpredictable nor sudden. “The transition is much more predictable than people give it credit for,” said [Sanmi Koyejo](https://cs.stanford.edu/~sanmi/), a computer scientist at Stanford and the paper’s senior author. “Strong claims of emergence have as much to do with the way we choose to measure as they do with what the models are doing.” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hss6j4296e8wg1sr1wzd8srh))
- The size is measured in terms of parameters, roughly analogous to all the ways that words can be connected. The more parameters, the more connections an LLM can find. GPT-2 had 1.5 billion parameters, while GPT-3.5, the LLM that powers ChatGPT, uses 350 billion. GPT-4, which debuted in March 2023 and now underlies [Microsoft Copilot](https://www.wired.com/story/microsoft-windows-11-copilot-generative-ai-assistant-tips/), reportedly uses 1.75 trillion. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hss78bppb3f3kkz9evcbp6kk))
- Stanford who cast emergence as a “mirage” recognize that LLMs become more effective as they scale up; in fact, [the added complexity](https://www.quantamagazine.org/new-theory-suggests-chatbots-can-understand-text-20240122/) of larger models should make it possible to get better at more difficult and diverse problems. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hss78z53yy35whb01sw4rc29))
- Either they could do it perfectly, or they couldn’t. So even if an LLM predicted most of the digits correctly, it failed. That didn’t seem right. If you’re calculating 100 plus 278, then 376 seems like a much more accurate answer than, say, −9.34. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hss7ttc1kv6bpr8n7xmv5efe))
- So instead, Koyejo and his collaborators tested the same task using a metric that awards partial credit. “We can ask: How well does it predict the first digit? Then the second? Then the third?” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hss7v1encfatqbh7fm4pd0ea))
- Together with Brando Miranda, another Stanford graduate student, they chose new metrics showing that as parameters increased, the LLMs predicted an increasingly correct sequence of digits in addition problems. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hss7vqqa8e6sdqb906w6xyfj))
- other scientists point out that the work doesn’t fully dispel the notion of emergence. For example, the trio’s paper doesn’t explain how to predict when metrics, or which ones, will show abrupt improvement in an LLM, said [Tianshi Li](https://tianshili.me/), a computer scientist at Northeastern University. “So in that sense, these abilities are still unpredictable,” she said. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hss7whjrwn10bgc6fat127nn))
- Others, such as Jason Wei, a computer scientist now at OpenAI who has compiled a list of emergent abilities and was an author on the BIG-bench paper, [have argued](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/common-arguments-regarding-emergent-abilities) that the earlier reports of emergence were sound because for abilities like arithmetic, the right answer really is all that matters. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hss7xznk05z86065trmwktya))
- even if emergence in today’s LLMs can be explained away by different measuring tools, it’s likely that won’t be the case for tomorrow’s larger, more complicated LLMs. “When we grow LLMs to the next level, inevitably they will borrow knowledge from other tasks and other models,” said [Xia “Ben” Hu](https://cs.rice.edu/~xh37/index.html), a computer scientist at Rice University. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01hss7z0c943ffj2k33fv3zbfy))
