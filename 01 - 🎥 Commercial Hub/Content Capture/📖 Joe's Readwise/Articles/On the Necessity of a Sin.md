## Metadata
- Author: [[Ethan Mollick]]
- Date: 2024-03-30
- Full Title: On the Necessity of a Sin
- Category: #articles
- Summary: The author discusses the concept of anthropomorphizing artificial intelligence (AI) and the risks associated with it, emphasizing the importance of understanding AI's limitations and treating it like an alien person rather than a human. The text also highlights the need for managing AI effectively, aligning with the idea of treating AI as a person to improve interactions and outcomes. Ultimately, the future of AI involves increasing anthropomorphism, which may impact human-AI relationships and interactions, emphasizing the need for safe and productive approaches.

## Highlights
- Anthropomorphism is the act of ascribing human characteristics to something that is nonhuman. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htgdaw7gk8yg348ce19eck1k))
- It’s no surprise, then, that we’re tempted to anthropomorphize artificial intelligence, especially since talking to LLMs feels so much like talking to a person. Even the developers and researchers who design these systems c[an fall into the trap of using humanlike terms](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21507740.2020.1740350) to describe their creations, starting with labels like “machine learning.” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htgdb9evw4jtmevd10dwfh7m))
- a lot of [researchers are deeply concerned](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/stop-treating-ai-models-like-people) about the implications of casually acting as if AI is a human, both ethically and epistemologically. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htgds9k24cp7fqaqfy58r40v))
- Current AI systems don’t have a consciousness, emotions, a sense of self, or physical sensations. So why take the risk? Because as imperfect as the analogy is, working with AI is easiest if you think of it like an alien person rather than a human-built machine. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htgdt540b9wj14asb023k06j))
- IT departments are often put in charge of corporate AI strategy, computer scientists are assumed to be experts on forecasting the social change AI might bring, and, most importantly - many people seem reluctant to use AI because they “don’t know computer stuff.” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htgdtvry3fb8xpwchqc98p4w))
- LLMs are made of software, but they do not work like most software. They are probabilistic and mostly unpredictable, producing different outcomes given the same input. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htgdvrabah88xm61k4t8dsd8))
- Though they don’t think, they produce simulations of human language and thought that are, as far as we can tell, [original enough that they out-invent most humans](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/automating-creativity). The are perceived as [more empathetic, and more accurate, than human doctors in controlled trials.](https://blog.research.google/2024/01/amie-research-ai-system-for-diagnostic_12.html) And yet they are also limited in odd ways that are surprising, like an [inability to do backward reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12288). ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htgdw80wjftmh1mqbjfyn13y))
- treat AI as if it were human because, in many ways, it behaves like one. This mindset can significantly improve your understanding of how and when to use AI in a practical, if not technical, sense. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htgdxs34msvmz2r6fn8tgrgp))
- This quasi-human weirdness is why the best users of AI are often managers and teachers, people who can understand the perspective of others and correct it when it is going wrong. You will notice, for example, in the conversation above, that I cut off the AI (using the “stop” button) when I saw it was going in the wrong direction and offered both feedback and correction. Rather than focusing purely on teaching people to write good prompts, we might want to spend more time teaching them to manage the AI. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htgdzjjwv1ecsb07yqz115mc))
- The idea of treating AI like a person also aligns with the two of the best basic prompting techniques. The first is to give the AI a specific persona, defining who the AI is and what problems it should tackle. Telling the system “who” it is helps shape the outputs of the system. Telling it to *act as a teacher of MBA students* will result in a different output than if you ask it to *act as a circus clown*. This isn’t magical—you can’t say *Act as Bill Gate*s and get better business advice or *write like Hemingway* and get amazing prose —but it can help make the tone and direction appropriate for your purpose. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htge0g0c26yndrbj0stfejen))
- A second powerful technique that [aligns with treating the AI as a person is chain-of-thought prompting, where you ask the AI to “think step by step” or provide clear instructions for it to follow.](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/captains-log-the-irreducible-weirdness) ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htge0r9dqwms268t9zpe9x6k))
- And some of the risks of AI might actually be lower if their creators gave them more obvious fake personalities. You aren’t used to a computer making errors, but you know not to put complete trust in Sydney, your slightly-over-the-top and stressed-out intern, no matter how useful they are. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01htge1h9zmt127r33aya9f31h))
